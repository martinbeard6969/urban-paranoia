# Modern pressures

Six present-day anxieties that run through *Urban Paranoia*, where each one sits in the Lodge's plan, how the characters live with it, and where the lines are.

**How these work in the story.** Each pressure is a real public debate with real people on several sides. The story **doesn't take a side in the real debate**. Its horror is that the Lodge **feeds every side at once**, so that the argument itself does the work: exhausted, suspicious, quiet people who never look up. The reader should finish the book understanding the fear on each side *and* seeing how the fear is being used.

Every pressure follows the hard rules in [CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md). Real-world news on these topics goes through the [news desk filter](../campaign/news-desk.md). Most of it is amber or red.

---

## 1. The AI race

**The real backdrop:** rapid, competitive AI development between companies and countries; uncertainty about jobs, control and safety; a sense that nobody is steering.

**In the Lodge's plan:** **Replace** (stage 5). The **Engine Seat** doesn't need to build AI. It only needs the race to happen *fast*, with no time to agree on rules, so that "there's no stopping it" becomes common sense. In the Tenants' counsel, a race is the perfect tool, because everyone running it believes they're the one choosing to run.

**New counsel (Minutes Fragment 8, book):**
> *LET THEM RACE. NO ONE WHO IS RUNNING LOOKS AT THE ROAD.*

**How characters live it:**
- **Vesper** lost her job to a routing system and doesn't know whether to fear the machine or the people who bought it.
- **Teo** works nights inside the data centre where the race is physically happening. He's the story's view of how fast, hungry and indifferent it is.
- **Danny's** view: "Nobody's racing for us. They're racing each other with our wages as the fuel."
- **Crane**, late in the book: "We built the tables for a world without work in 1974. I never thought anyone would *hurry*."

**Lines:**
- No real AI company, lab, executive or government is shown as part of the plan or as acting on the Lodge's behalf.
- No claims that a real AI system is controlled by anything.
- Real developments are backdrop only (news desk 🟢 for research and statistics; 🟡 for specific company announcements).

## 2. "Own nothing": wealth and the buying-up of assets

**The real backdrop:**
- Rising rents.
- Home ownership slipping out of reach for younger people.
- Large investors buying housing and other assets.
- More of daily life moving to subscriptions and rentals.
- **The phrase "you'll own nothing and be happy":** it comes from a 2016 speculative essay about a possible future city, later used in a World Economic Forum video. It has since become an online slogan for the fear that ownership is being taken away on purpose.

**In the Lodge's plan:** **Unsettle** (stage 3). The **Ledger** (Seat Four, Larkspire Mutual Re) and **Hearth** (Seat Seven, empty since Marguerite left) portfolios. People who own nothing can't put down roots, and people who can't put down roots don't build the networks the Lodge fears.

**New counsel (Minutes Fragment 6, Day 150):**
> *LET THEM RENT THE GROUND THEY STAND ON. A TENANT DOES NOT PLANT TREES.*

*(The Tenants call humanity "tenants" too. That's the joke, and the horror, of the name.)*

**In-world:**
- **Larkspire Mutual Re** quietly owns thousands of homes through property funds, including, it turns out, the block Vesper rents in. It's a fictional echo, and the Archivist reveals it in Phase 3.
- **The Mission Hall network's** answer in the book: a tool library, a food co-op and a community land trust. Owning *together* is the thing the Ledger never modelled.

**How the phrase is handled:**
- Characters may hear and repeat the online slogan. Vesper can say it sounds like her stamp.
- **Danny corrects the record** in the story: it came from a think-piece imagining a future, not from a plan, and the real problem is ordinary: "rich people buying houses because houses make money."
- **The story never says or implies that the World Economic Forum, or any real organisation or person, is part of the plan.** The real organisation is not used, named or imitated in in-world content beyond Danny's factual correction.

**Lines:**
- Real data on housing and wealth: news desk 🟢 (official statistics, research).
- Named real investment firms or billionaires: 🔴. Never shown acting in the story.

## 3. Watched: CCTV and surveillance

**The real backdrop:** spreading CCTV, live facial recognition trials, data collection by companies and states, and ongoing public argument about safety versus privacy.

**In the Lodge's plan:** the **Ward Seat** (Twelve). The Lodge doesn't need to watch everyone. It only needs everyone to **feel** watched. People who feel watched go quiet, and quiet is what the Tenants want.

**In-world:**
- The Coyle's archive unit's cameras and keycards, logged through the routing server, are how the Lodge watches Vesper (canon decision D5).
- **Cobb** never needs to follow Vesper. He just reads her day back to her.
- Teo's data centre stores footage for a fictional city contract. He notices the Lodge's racks pull a copy.

**Lines:**
- No real police force, council, company or surveillance product is shown working for the Lodge.
- Real deployments and statistics: news desk 🟢 for broad figures and reports; 🟡 for specific local deployments (fictionalise them).
- **No real-world call to action** (rule 5): characters never encourage readers to tamper with, map or confront real cameras or the people operating them.

## 4. Hush: speech, hate-speech laws and blasphemy

**The real backdrop:**
- Live public arguments about where free expression ends.
- Laws against hate speech and online harms, and how they're enforced.
- Debates in some countries over whether rules protecting religions from insult amount to "blasphemy laws by the back door".
- People on every side feel silenced, some by laws and some by abuse and threats.

**In the Lodge's plan:** a new strand inside **Divide**, nicknamed **"the Hush"** in Lodge papers. The **Signal Seat** (Three) and the **Faith Seat** (One) work **both sides at once**:
1. they amplify the most hateful speech, so that people demand protection;
2. then they amplify fear of the protections, so that people feel censored;
3. and they make sure every religious and secular community believes the other is coming for it.

The result: everyone is angry, nobody feels safe to speak, and people choose silence. In the Tenants' terms, the field goes *still*.

**New counsel (Minutes Fragment 7, alongside the decoy counsel):**
> *LET EACH SIDE FEAR THE OTHERS MOUTH. THEN NEITHER WILL OPEN ITS OWN.*

**In-world:**
- @longweather_ (Phase 4 reveal) is shown to have run accounts on *both* sides of a speech row. The same no-"I" writing style appears in both.
- **Pastor Ama** holds open meetings at the Mission Hall where people with opposite views on the issue are made to sit at the same table, which the Hush is designed to stop.
- **Vesper's blog moderation** becomes a plot point: she deletes scapegoating and gets accused of censorship. She has to explain the difference between protecting people and protecting ideas, in her own words.

**Lines:**
- The story **doesn't argue for or against any real law, bill, court case or politician.** Specific cases are news desk 🔴.
- **No religion or religious group is shown as behind any law or any part of the plan.** The Faith Seat is a single fictional chair that works on *all* faiths and on secular people too.
- Broad, neutral data (e.g. surveys on whether people feel free to speak) may be 🟢. Write it with both sides represented.

## 5. Belonging: the argument over multiculturalism and integration

**The real backdrop:** real, heated public argument in many Western countries about immigration levels, integration and social cohesion. Some describe multiculturalism as a "failed experiment"; others point to communities that work well and to research showing mixed pictures. It is one of the most heated debates of the moment.

**In the Lodge's plan:** this is the **Border Seat's** (Six) main work, and the clearest case of the **Decoy Doctrine**. The canon is fixed and **must not be inverted** (CLAUDE.md, the central twist):
- **Migrants are not part of the plan.** They're among its targets, like everyone else.
- The Lodge **did not plan migration**. It plans the **argument about migration**: it feeds the "failed experiment" story *and* the opposite story, to whoever will be most angered by each.
- The aim is for neighbours to see each other as the problem. Then nobody notices thirteen chairs.

**How characters live it (with real feeling on more than one side):**
- **Danny's neighbourhood** is mixed, and it has real tensions over housing, school places and changes people didn't vote for. Danny himself has said things he's not proud of. The story **takes those worries seriously** as human feelings, without endorsing blame of any group.
- **Pastor Ama's congregation** includes long-time residents and newcomers, some of whom feel unwelcome.
- **The turn (book Act 2–3):** Vesper and Danny trace a local flare-up back to a Larkspire letting decision and @longweather_ posts. The fight was *engineered on top of* real pressures. The pressures were real; the target was fake.
- **The Mission Hall network** is deliberately mixed. Its strength is that it's the one thing the Border Seat can't split.

**Lines (strictest of all five):**
- **Never** present any ethnic, religious, national or migrant group as a problem, a threat, or an instrument of the plan (rules 1 and 9).
- **Never** frame migration or multiculturalism as an elite plan or "experiment" designed to replace or weaken anyone. That's the real-world "Great Replacement" conspiracy theory, which has motivated real violence, and the story exists to undermine it.
- In-world characters may *voice* the "failed experiment" view as **their opinion**, in character, and be answered by others in the story. The narrative never confirms it.
- News about migration, asylum, integration incidents or specific groups is **🔴 always** for news-desk use. Only broad, neutral social-cohesion surveys can be 🟡, and only for writers'-room background.

## 6. The outrage engine: polarisation, extremism and the culture war

**The real backdrop:** daily life in many Western countries now runs through a constant culture war. It covers:
- extremism on the far left and far right
- the fight over "woke" and "anti-woke" politics
- fierce disputes over identity, race, gender, policing, war and protest, and immigration enforcement
- media and feeds that reward whoever is angriest

People on every side feel unheard and under attack.

**How the story handles it: invented flashpoints, never real movements.**
The story does **not** depict, name or comment on real movements, causes or conflicts (for example, real racial-justice movements, transgender people's rights, real wars or the protests about them, or real immigration enforcement). Two reasons:
1. **Putting a real movement inside the Lodge's plan says it isn't genuine:** that it's astroturfed, a psy-op, or an elite "agenda". That's an existing real-world conspiracy claim about real people, and it breaks rules 1, 2 and 9.
2. **Several of these involve real deaths and real vulnerable groups** (rule 4).

Instead, the story's city has **its own invented flashpoints** that capture the *experience* without mapping onto any one real dispute. They must not read as a retelling of a real event.

**The Outrage Engine in the Lodge's plan:** the **Signal Seat** (Three) runs it with **Ward** (Twelve) and **Border** (Six). It's the Decoy Doctrine at street level. It finds a real local grievance, then feeds each side the version that will most enrage it. Fictional Steward-run accounts (@longweather_'s network) push opposite sides of the *same* fight.

### Invented flashpoints (story's city only)

| Flashpoint | What happens | What it lets the story explore |
|---|---|---|
| **The Wharf Road scheme** | A housing redevelopment pits long-time renters, newer arrivals, a developer (secretly a Larkspire fund) and the council against each other | Housing anger, "outsiders", and who actually profits |
| **The Ferris Library row** | A talk at the local library is cancelled after complaints, then reinstated after counter-complaints, and both sides receive threats | Free speech vs offence; how fast a small decision becomes a national story |
| **The rumour** | An online rumour about a crime that **never happened**, blamed on whichever group each audience already fears. The versions differ by feed. | How rumours start pile-ons. Vesper traces it to Lodge accounts and nobody was hurt, but it was close. |
| **Coyle's "respect at work" training** | A mandatory course at Coyle's that some staff welcome and others resent | The "woke" argument at work, shown from both sides with sympathy (see below) |

### Extremism (left and right)

- **Two fictional online groups** sit at opposite ends. They're **invented** and are **not** stand-ins for any real organisation:
  - **Clearfell** (left-coded) claims to fight for "the people being priced out". The name is a forestry term for cutting down every tree in an area at once. Members take it to mean clearing out landlords and investors and starting over.
  - **Frostline** (right-coded) claims to defend "the people being replaced". The name is the depth below which the ground freezes. Members take it to mean a line that holds.
  - **The fair-play clue:** both names come from the Lodge's own land and weather vocabulary (pruning, fields, the long weather). When both groups' recruiters are traced to the same rented office in Phase 4, the matching names are one more giveaway. Crane's Harlow-Vane papers can use the word "clearfell".
  - **Never** give either group a logo, symbol, slogan, manifesto or recruitment copy that could be lifted and used for real.
- **The twist at street level:** both groups' most active recruiters turn out to be Steward-run accounts in the same rented office. The Outrage Engine radicalises in both directions because extremists on each side are the best advertisement for the other.
- **A human arc: Jordan Pryce** (see [characters](characters.md)), a young man from Danny's estate, is pulled into **Clearfell** after losing his apprenticeship and seeing his mum priced out. He's brought back out by the Mission Hall network: by people, not arguments.
- **Lines:**
  - No manifestos, slogans, recruitment copy or symbols that could be lifted and used.
  - No methods of violence or attack planning.
  - Extremist views are shown through their effects on people, not given persuasive airtime.
  - Neither side's extremism is excused.

### "Woke" and the backlash

**The real backdrop:** "woke" began as a call to stay alert to injustice. It is now mostly used as an insult for progressive politics around race, gender, identity and language. The two sides are:
- people who feel lectured, policed or afraid to speak by new norms
- people who feel those norms finally protect them, and who see "anti-woke" as a cover for cruelty

Each side feels the other has power over its daily life.

**In the Lodge's plan:** the Outrage Engine feeds **"woke" and "anti-woke" outrage at the same time**. Neither side is the Lodge's creation. Both are real feelings held by real people. The Lodge just keeps every row at boiling point and makes sure each side only ever sees the other at its worst.

**How characters live it:**
- **Vesper:** she found the Coyle's "respect at work" course clumsy and patronising, *and* remembers the colleague it was written for, who'd been mocked for years. She holds both thoughts at once, and gets attacked from both sides when she says so on the blog.
- **Danny:** a former union rep who sat through a hundred of these courses. He thinks most were corporate box-ticking to avoid lawsuits, *and* that the workplace really was crueller before them. His line: "Calling it woke or calling it bigot — either way, you've stopped talking to the bloke next to you. That's the point."
- **Pastor Ama:** her congregation contains people on both sides. She keeps them in the same room.
- **The Ferris Library row** (above) is the story's main "woke vs anti-woke" flashpoint.

**Lines:**
- No real campaigns, commentators, politicians, companies, trainers or policies are depicted or parodied.
- Neither side is shown as the plan's instrument, and no identity or group is shown as the problem.
- Characters can use the word "woke" and argue about it in character. The narrative doesn't take a side, only shows the fight being *used*.

### Rules for this section (strictest in the bible)

- **Never** name, depict, parody or allude to a real movement, cause, protest, war or conflict as part of the plot, as Lodge-made, or as Lodge-amplified.
- **Never** imply any real social movement or identity is fake, engineered or an "agenda".
- **Never** present trans people, Black people, Palestinians, Israelis, Jewish or Muslim people, migrants (with or without legal status), or any other real group as a problem, threat or instrument.
- Invented flashpoints must stay **recognisably fictional**: invented places, names and details, and no one-to-one mapping to a real event.
- Characters may hold and voice strong opinions in character, and other characters answer them. The narrative never confirms a culture-war position as the truth. The only truth it confirms is that the fighting is being *used*.

## Where the pressures meet (book Act 2 centrepiece)

Full outline: [book/act-2/scene-the-wall.md](../book/act-2/scene-the-wall.md).


In the book, Vesper pins the six pressures to her wall. Danny adds the names of their neighbours under each one. Then they see it: **every pressure turns people towards each other in suspicion, and away from the thirteen chairs.** That's the scene where the twist lands for the reader.

| Pressure | Lodge Seat(s) | What it takes from people | The Mission Hall's answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI race | Engine (5) | Work, and the sense that anyone's steering | Skills swaps; teaching each other |
| Own nothing | Ledger (4), Hearth (7) | Roots | Owning together |
| Watched | Ward (12) | Privacy, and the nerve to act | Meeting face to face |
| Hush | Signal (3), Faith (1) | Voice | Disagreeing at the same table |
| Belonging | Border (6) | Neighbours | A mixed network the Border Seat can't split |
| Outrage engine | Signal (3), Ward (12), Border (6) | Calm, and the young | People, not arguments, bringing someone back |
