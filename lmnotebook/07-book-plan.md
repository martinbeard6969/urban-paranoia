# Urban Paranoia: synopsis

*by Martin Defries*

*For the long weather.*

---

In October 1962, a week before the world would hold its breath over a handful of missiles, seven junior officials from seven countries sat down to dinner in an English country house nobody can find any more. They'd been invited because they were clever. They'd been chosen because nobody would miss them. There was a telephone planner from Ohio who resented his boss, a West German statistician who checked everything twice, and a British demographer who wrote down what other people only noticed. Four more were there too. In a small panelled room beyond the library, a new teletype stood unplugged against the wall.

At ten past two in the morning it began to type.

*SIT. THERE IS TIME.*

Four of the guests left by breakfast, unwell and unclear, and spent the rest of their lives failing to remember a dream. Three stayed. They called what had spoken to them **the Tenants**, because it never said what it was, only that it had been here a long time and meant to stay. It did not threaten. It did not ask to be worshipped. It advised, in the calm, patient voice of a farmer talking about soil: *A FIELD THAT IS CROWDED FIGHTS ITSELF. LET IT.* And it was never once wrong about what was coming.

Sixty years later the three are dead, and the circle they founded, **the Table**, has twelve members on five continents and a thirteenth chair that nobody sits in. They were nobodies once. Now, quietly and through layers of lawyers who don't know why their instructions are so prescient, their families hold pieces of the companies that run the world: the phone by your bed, the warehouse that brings your shopping, the car that drives itself, and both of the laboratories racing to build a mind. They are not in charge of those companies. They never needed to be. They just knew, decades early, which way the weather would turn, and they made sure it kept turning.

**Nell Okafor-Reyes** didn't know any of this. She was a freight analyst, a "human spreadsheet with opinions", until a routing system did her job better and her team of nine became a team of one. Clearing out her old firm's archive in the last weeks of her notice, she finds a 1971 shipping manifest with no weight, no charge, a date that isn't a date, and four words in smudged purple ink: **FOR THE LONG WEATHER.** She starts a blog under the name Vesper, because writing things down is the only skill she has left that a machine hasn't been paid to do better.

Her readers crack the date. A retired driver remembers cash-in-hand runs to a house that no longer has an address, and a cabinet that hummed with the power off. Then a typed letter arrives at a flat she has never told anyone about. It comes from someone who has carried a dead woman's papers for thirty-eight years. The letter knows her password, her father's birthday, and *why*. The teletype that left that house in 1971 and the server that replaced Nell in 2025 weigh exactly the same. The letter tells her: *Do not blame anyone you can see from your window. That is what they want.*

The trail runs through a dusty research institute's card index to **Hollis Crane**, an old economist who once sat beside the machine and did the arithmetic it implied. He seems remorseful and helpful. He is also the Table's best hope of finding out what Nell knows. Through him, and through the scattered pages of the Harrowgate Minutes, she learns the shape of the plan: unmoor people from faith and place; break their unions; keep them anxious and renting; give every fear a human face; replace their work; and let their numbers fall without anyone lifting a hand. *THERE IS MORE TIME THAN THERE ARE PEOPLE.*

Against this stands almost nothing. There's **Danny Kessler**, a former union rep who now delivers parcels for a five-star rating. He thinks it's all ordinary greed, and he's usually right. There's **Pastor Ama**, who keeps her hall open to people who'd rather not sit together. There's a night technician called **Teo** who keeps finding twelve-by-twelve grids in errors that shouldn't exist. And there's **Jack Prentice**, a nineteen-year-old who calls himself Jordan, lost his apprenticeship to the same machine as Nell, and is being courted online by people who promise him someone to blame.

The book's midpoint is a wall in Nell's flat at four in the morning. On it are six index cards for six pressures: the race, the rent, the cameras, the silence, the arguments over who belongs, the rage. Under each one Danny writes the names of their neighbours: the people it's hurting on one side, and the people being blamed for it on the other. The same names keep appearing on both sides, and every thread runs sideways. There's nowhere on the wall to pin the people who built it.

Then Nell's phone lights up. Her landlord has scheduled an inspection. She has never heard its name before, and she has seen it everywhere since.

What follows is a race of a quieter kind. There's the Table's slow tightening around her: a polite fixer who recites her day back to her, an anonymous account that never once uses the word *I*. There's her father's file, which says he asked no questions until the year he walked away. And there's Crane's choice, made too late and made anyway. He brings her the workbooks he filled in for fifty years and tells her what they were forecasting. It was never a trend. It was a **date**.

The truth waits in a warehouse at the edge of the city, where the old teletype sits beside a chair saved from a fire. In its safe, under the Harrowgate Minutes, is something far older: a ledger in a dead script, counted from a different Day 0, written in the same farming voice and still never saying *I*. *We weren't the first Table.*

The Tenants are not masters. They are **tenant farmers**. They have held the lease on this world since before anyone wrote anything down, and they have always worked through the same kind of person: clerks, scribes, census-takers, counters, the overlooked. The first thing anyone ever wrote down was who owed what. Humanity built everything itself; the Tenants only kept the books. What we make is their harvest. What we are is their inventory, and for the first time in history the machines we are racing to build can finish it. They have kept the field alive the whole time, the way a farmer keeps soil alive, because you don't let a crop die before the harvest. That was why, in 1962, it would not burn. When the lease ends, the brightest are to be taken and the rest let go. The Table has served all this time because its families were promised places on that list. None of them understood that "the brightest" only ever meant the easiest to harvest.

And the Tenants are afraid. They pay rent too, to something no one has ever seen, and an inspection is coming. *THE HOUSE HAS BEEN EMPTY BEFORE. IT WAS QUIETER.*

It's the same shape all the way up: a ladder where nobody sees two rungs above them, nobody says *I*, everyone is promised a place higher up, and everyone fears the inspection. Nell has seen that shape before, in a text at four in the morning from a landlord she'd never heard of.

So when every tenant on Wharf Road receives the same message (*routine inspection of your home scheduled; no action required*), someone in the Mission Hall asks the only question the ladder has never prepared for: *What if nobody opens the door?*

Nobody does. Forty flats refuse the inspection and withhold their rent together, and people who stood on opposite sides of Nell's wall sign the same letter. She posts the method with no names and no villains, and it spreads to every street where the same landlord owns homes, on four continents, until the Table's money stalls and its decoys have nobody left to turn against each other. There's no riot and no burning: only tenants, on every rung, refusing to be inspected.

The book does not end with the Table destroyed or the lease broken. It ends with a hall too full for its chairs, where strangers go round the room each saying a sentence that begins with *I*. It ends with Nell remembering that every age has had its peasants' revolt, and that the ledger has never been finished because the people at the bottom keep looking sideways. And it ends, at ten past two on the sixty-fourth anniversary, with a word on a machine that has never before written anything but advice. It is the first sign that the Tenants have seen something they cannot harvest, and are wondering whether tenants can refuse their landlord too:

*INTERESTING.*

- **The Lodge is now multinational, and was mid-level before contact** (see [the-lodge.md](../bible/the-lodge.md)): the seven guests, the Table's rise, the families, and the three layers of command (Seats → Factors → Stewards).
- **The Table's wealth sits in fictional global companies:** Pellucid (devices), Tollgate (marketplace and cloud), Halvard Dynamics (EVs, batteries, satellites), and the rival AI labs Nimfold and Lanternwork (see [locations & organisations](../bible/locations-and-organisations.md)). Their founders and staff don't know the Lodge exists.
- **v3 adds the deep canon** (see [the-tenants.md](../bible/the-tenants.md), [deep-time.md](../bible/deep-time.md), [the-ladder.md](../bible/the-ladder.md)): the lease, the harvest and inventory, the Taken, the earlier Tables, the inspection, and the rent-strike ending (outline ch 25–30).
- **Safeguards observed** (canon decisions D11–D13): the cull's method is never shown and appears here only as "the rest let go"; the Taken are "the easiest to harvest", never a group; humans built everything; the revolt is the invented, lawful Larkspire rent strike, and real riots are never named.
- **Style target:** the restraint of literary SF: calm sentences, concrete detail, dread from implication, and humour kept close to the grief. Comparable reading: Le Guin's clarity, Ishiguro's withholding, the procedural chill of *Annihilation*, and the slow-burn paranoia of *The Parallax View*. Every chapter should earn one line a reader would underline.

# Urban Paranoia: chapter outline

**Shape:** 30 chapters in three acts, plus a short epilogue. Present-day chapters (spring–autumn 2026) are in **Nell's first person**. Seven **interludes** step back into the Table's history in close third person, each a short, self-contained story that shifts how the reader sees the present. The book opens on **Day 0** (14 Oct 1962) and closes on the 64th anniversary, **Day 23,376** (Wed 14 Oct 2026).

**The spine:** Nell moves from *collecting evidence* (Act 1), to *seeing the shape* (Act 2, ending at the Wall), to *acting with other people* (Act 3). The Table is never defeated. It's **surprised**.

**Drafting priorities:** a sinister, compelling plot; variable pacing; memorable characters; at the standard of a Nebula Award-winning novella (full rules in `CLAUDE.md`, Voice & style).

**Pacing rule:** every chapter knows its speed. **Slow** stretches disclose (history, a document, Crane's context, an interlude). **Fast** stretches thrill: action, less information, short paragraphs, nothing explained until afterwards, if at all. Alternate them across the book and within chapters, and never let two slow chapters run back to back in the present day. **Slow never means dull** (Martin, 28 Oct 2026): every slow stretch carries a live question, a threat or a wonder, and suspense, wonder or thrill is present on every page. Information is easy to understand; its meaning is hard to fathom. Act One's fast beats: the Coyle's keycard (ch 6), Graham's door (ch 7), the night at DC-3 and the bus stop (ch 10).

**Rhythm rule:** whenever the Tenants speak, long observed paragraphs are broken by single centred lines of counsel (as in chapter 1).

**Typography rule:** centred capitals (`>`) are reserved for the Tenants. Letters, documents, stamps and notes use `>>` (left-aligned, ruled). Counsel quoted inside a document is set as a document.

**Paper rule (Act One pacing, 28 Oct 2026):** in Act One only **two documents** appear on the page: the 1971 manifest (ch 2) and the Archivist's letter (ch 6). Everything else Nell finds through people, places, her own skill and her senses: what someone remembers, what she does, what she hears. Logs, screenshots and enclosures are told in a line of her working, never reproduced. The ARG keeps its scans and puzzles, because the blog is where Nell posts her paper (see `bible/open-questions.md`, Settled).

**Cobb before he speaks:** Cobb appears three times, unnamed, before ch 10: at the next table in Len's café (ch 3), at the wheel of the bird-logo van (ch 5), and walking a dog past Nell's window (ch 6). Each time he's described too plainly to stick on first reading; on rereading he's obvious. In ch 10 he lets her know it was him each time.

**Narrator labels:** every chapter opens with a small-capitals line under its title saying **who is narrating and when**. Nell's chapters give her name, the Lodge day-count and the real date; interludes give the viewpoint character's **role** (Founders stay unnamed), the day-count where it matters, and the place and date. If the viewpoint changes inside a chapter, a section label marks it. So does a jump in time the reader would otherwise have to count (e.g. `**SATURDAY 23 MAY**`). Format in the chapter file: a bold, all-capitals block, e.g. `**NELL · DAY 23,220 · MONDAY 11 MAY 2026**`.

**12. Forty Pages**
A parcel: the surviving Minutes fragments, with the Archivist's note *NOW YOU HAVE WHAT I HAVE.* Nell reads them in one night: *LET THEM RENT THE GROUND THEY STAND ON*, *LET THEM RACE*, *GIVE EVERY FEAR A FACE THAT IS NOT OURS*. The plan has a shape now. At 4 a.m., reading about the oldest writing in the world to stay awake, she learns a real fact: some of the earliest writing was accounting, tallies of grain and labour. *"The first thing anyone ever wrote down was who owed what."* (Real backdrop only; never claimed as the Tenants' doing.) *Ends:* she reads her own tenancy agreement and laughs until it frightens her. *Missing knock (D14):* *GIVE EVERY FEAR A FACE THAT IS NOT OURS* is the counsel the strand runs on. *The voice's first possessive (fixes v1.5 C1a):* Nell notices, in one line and with no explanation, that *NOT OURS* is the only time the voice owns anything, and what it owns is not being blamed. (Progression: no *I* or *we* in 1962, then *OURS*, then the epilogue's *I*.) *Act One pacing:* the parcel now also carries **Fragment Four** and **Marguerite's 1987 note**, held back from ch 7. *The line (D15):* **call 1.** At 02:10, mid-way through the fragments, she sees the old phone box on her road lit, with the man from the bus stop inside. She goes down in her socks and listens from behind the box: *"Wharf Road's quieter… She's reading. Tonight. All of it… Yes. Thinner."* Hiss with the rhythm of speech, no words. *"Understood."* A fast beat in a slow chapter; then she reads until four.

**13. The Ferris Library**

The **Hush** at street level: everyone afraid to speak, for opposite reasons. *Ends:* she notices that threats against both sides used the same odd phrasing. *Missing knock (D14):* **death 1:** the old man with the tartan flask (ch 5) is found after a fall, three days late. **14. A Long-Standing Gift**
With Crane's help Nell traces Larkspire's stakes: **Pellucid, Tollgate, Halvard Dynamics, Nimfold, Lanternwork**. These are quiet minority holdings, and the companies' founders know nothing. The scale lands on her: the phone in her hand, the cloud her old routing system runs on, *both* AI labs. *"LET THEM RACE."* Crane's tenderness for her is growing real, and so is his fear. *Missing knock (D14):* **Crane's slip:** over tea, he mentions a detail of death 1 that Nell hadn't told him. She notices, and lets it go.

**15. Interlude: The Factors** *(present day, the Table)*
A family office somewhere warm. A Factor briefs Seat Four (about 80, a Canadian, crisp) on the blog. Seat Four's granddaughter works at Lanternwork and believes in it. **Mother Quill**, 96, at her farm in New Zealand. Her line on the recording (Q-2): *"Leave her be. Let her be the one who explains it. People believe the ones who doubt themselves."* Seat Eleven (Commons), a Gardener, says nothing and writes it down. *Missing knock (D14):* one line in the Factor's briefing, among other figures: *welfare-check rates down eleven per cent, Ward reports.* Nobody reacts. Nobody orders, causes or welcomes a death. *The line (D15):* another line in the same briefing, in Cobb's words: *Wharf Road quieter.* The reader recognises it from ch 12; nobody at the Table does. Kept apart from the welfare-check line, which is Ward's.

**16. Clearfell**
Jack is drawn into **Clearfell**: anger at landlords, a recruiter who listens. Danny finds out. Frostline posts blame Jack's estate. Two groups, two enemies, same tone. Nell and Danny can't reach him. *Ends:* Jack: "At least they're *doing* something." *Missing knock (D14):* **death 2:** someone on the Wharf Road estate. Frostline's posts, which already blame Jack's estate, now have a death to blame. Danny mentions he was at that block that week. Jack's slide toward Clearfell speeds up. The ending is unchanged.

**17. The Rumour**
A crime that never happened, a different villain in every feed, and "Jordan Pryce" named in one. A crowd outside the Mission Hall. Danny in the middle, hearing himself say something (never quoted). Ama holds the door. Nobody is hurt, but it was close. *Ends:* Danny walks home without his bike and doesn't notice. **Ama's back room** (set up in ch 5, where she quietly takes in the young woman in sunglasses) becomes the refuge during the confrontation: the people the rumour targets are sheltered there while Ama holds the door. *Missing knock (D14):* **death 3:** someone on the plywood-window family's street. **The "crime that never happened" is the killings:** "three, all on our side". **Jack's** Jordan account posts it before the local news. **Cobb** (or the bird-logo Larkspire van) is seen on that street the day before. **Nell posts** *"Three streets, one pattern?"* (curious, not blaming) and @longweather_ shares it with its own spin. Then the beats above. The rumour and the crowd echo no specific real event (D14).

**18. The Wall** *(MIDPOINT)*
Six cards, the counsel, the names, the threads that only run sideways. "Where's their name?" The crossed chair above the row. Confessions. 3:40 a.m.: **Larkspire Property Services** will inspect her flat. Jack Prentice and Jordan Pryce: *"one name for the feeds, one for the paperwork."* She texts Ama to book the hall. → [scene outline](act-2/scene-the-wall.md) *Missing knock (D14):* Nell pins up the three deaths and **her own people**: Danny, Ama, Jack, Crane, Cobb, and her own post. When she maps who *didn't* knock, every arrow runs sideways, at a neighbour. She sees she has done what the letter warned against. **Danny and Ama are explained in the Confessions** (the app's door timer; the key, which the old man asked for back because the feeds had made him afraid of visitors).

**19. Routine Inspection**
10:00. Two men from Larkspire "property services". One of them is Cobb, who admires her wall, which she didn't take down. He leaves a card with no name on it. Then the Archivist's post: **card 0212**. *Reliable. Asks no questions. Declined further work following S.7 matter.* Her father **stopped**. That night she opens his crossword dictionary (closed unread in ch 6): the small pencil numbers in its margins are **Lodge day-counts**, the runs he phoned through, kept privately. After the last one there is no number, only a single word he looked up and underlined (word to be found in drafting). He counted too, and he stopped. And: *Daughter: Coyle's analyst post. Placement noted.* Was her whole career arranged? *Ends:* she can't tell, and that's the worst of it.

## ACT THREE: INTERESTING (chapters 21–30, ~22,000–26,000 words)

**21. Interlude: Doctrine** *(2014)*
The Signal and Border Seats formalise the Decoy Doctrine from a 1963 counsel line. In 2022 a team of Stewards in a rented office opens an account called @longweather_. Their text is drafted by a system trained on fifty years of Lodge briefings, and it has never been told not to say "I". It just never does. *Function:* tells the reader the secret one beat before Nell finds it.

**22. Discredit**
Seat Four orders the blog destroyed. Fabricated "leaks" make Nell look like a crank, then a bigot. The Hush turns on her. Readers leave and her inbox fills with hate. Danny reads every message aloud and answers each with "Not her." *Ends:* she nearly deletes the blog. Crane, unasked, posts a single comment under his own name: *"She is right about the cards. I wrote some of them."* *Missing knock (D14):* the smear campaign uses Nell's ch 17 post as evidence that she's a crank. *The line (D15):* **call 2.** Same box, same hour; she's waiting this time, recording on her phone. He reports her readers leaving. Mid-report he stops for three seconds, his first hesitation, then: *"She's still there."*

**23. No One Is Speaking**
Jack, back at Danny's door, shows them his Clearfell recruiter's messages beside Frostline's. **Neither ever uses "I".** Nor does @longweather_. Teo traces all three to racks rented through Larkspire. The keycard logs (D5): the 144 lb server was watching her all along. *"It was the new teletype."* Nell exposes @longweather_ with evidence, no names, no villains but a machine and an office. Jack leaves Clearfell. *Missing knock (D14):* Teo traces the rumour's posts to the same Larkspire racks, which proves someone wrote the pattern. **Jack is explained:** his recruiter fed him death 3 first. *The line (D15):* Teo runs Nell's recording of call 2. The hiss has the same signature as the Larkspire racks. **Nobody was on the line**: for years Cobb has been reporting to the machine.

**24. Interlude: The Listener** *(the farm, September 2026)*
Mother Quill is dying. For the first time in fifty years the counsel doesn't come to her. Her heirs are gentle, rich and unaware. In the Ashby store, an unplugged teletype, silent since 1971, prints a single line that only a Factor's security camera sees: *THE CHAIR IS NOT A REWARD.* Succession panic at the Table.

**25. Model 7**
Crane comes to Nell's flat with a box: the original **Model 7** workbooks, the Table's goals written as arithmetic. He confesses he was sent to handle her, and that he stopped doing it weeks ago without telling anyone. The Archivist steps out of the dark: **Ruth Aldane**, 57. Crane tells her the truth about her mother's death: an accident the Table let everyone believe was murder. Ruth's life of fear was manufactured too. Among the workbook's columns (wages, rents, birth rates) is one headed **"U.P."**, with a pencil note in Crane's young hand: *U.P. — urban paranoia. Mutual suspicion between neighbours, per thousand households.* It has climbed every year since 1974. Crane: *"We called it urban paranoia. It was the only number we ever wanted to go up."* **This is the book's single use of its title phrase.** Model 7 wasn't modelling a trend. It forecasts a **date**: the end of the lease, the harvest and the cull. Crane names **the list**: the Taken, promised to the Seats' families, "the brightest and most attractive", meaning the most harvestable. *Ends:* *"I counted the wrong thing for fifty years. I counted people. I should have counted the people who stayed."* *Missing knock (D14):* **Crane is explained:** he was briefed as her handler. The U.P. column now has a cost the reader has seen. It mirrors Ruth's mother, the accident the Table let everyone believe was murder. *Hidden engine (D16), optional hint:* the list in Model 7 is headed **KEPT BACK**, a farmer's phrase the Seats read as rescue. Nobody comments on it.

**26. The Ashby Store**
Crane's keycard still works (*Weather status: under review*, never revoked). Nell, Ruth, Crane and Teo go inside: the teletype, the Exchange, two copies of the Minutes in a safe, and the thirteenth chair, saved from the fire. The teletype is printing. To them. It says nothing threatening. It asks nothing. It notes, politely, that they have come.

In the safe, beneath the Minutes, Ruth finds **the older ledger**: a bound book in a dead script, dated by a different day-count that began long before 1962, with the same farming language and the same absence of "I". Crane goes white. *"We weren't the first Table."* Beside the ledger are **the demographer's day-count notebooks**, from *Day 0* to her death in 2001. Nell, a reconciler, reads the last one. Near its final page, among the numbers, is one line that isn't a number: *"The Frenchman was the lucky one. He closed the lid."* (The Frenchman's thread, from ch 1 and 4, is paid off: he was the only one who got away clean. It echoes Len's "lucky" about Terry in ch 3.) The tenancy is far older than the Lodge, and earlier circles were promised the same list. Nobody knows what became of them. Then the teletype prints the line from Fragment 9 that Crane has never been allowed to read aloud: *THE HOUSE HAS BEEN EMPTY BEFORE. IT WAS QUIETER.* For the first time the four understand what the Tenants are: **tenant farmers, paying rent, afraid of an inspection.** ([deep time](../bible/deep-time.md), [the ladder](../bible/the-ladder.md)) *Hidden engine (D16), optional hint:* Nell sees that the older ledger's names are set out like a stock list, under the same heading, *KEPT BACK*, each with a mark she can't read. She doesn't say what she thinks it means, and the book never does.

**The full reveal (this is where Book 1 explains the aliens).** Nell asks the machine directly what it is. It doesn't answer. It types what it *does*, the way an agent describes a role: *THE HOUSE IS LET. THE HOUSE IS KEPT. THE RENT IS PAID.* Crane translates, slowly, from fifty years of listening. They aren't masters. They're **the managing agents**: they hold the lease, work the field, keep the books, and answer to someone above them that they have never seen. Nell, remembering a bird logo on a rent statement, says the line the chapter is built to land: *"How many renters ever meet the landlord? You deal with the agents."* Teo realises the 12×12 grid is the inventory's format: the field, counted in rows. *The line (D15):* one row is her street, and its cells hold words, not numbers: *quieter*, *thinner*, *still there*. Cobb's words. Ruth realises why the machine never says "I": *a managing agent never says "mine".* **The Tenants are confirmed real** (the Mirror theory is retired). What stays unknown is who, or what, they answer to. See the [resolution map](resolution-map.md).

**27. Interlude: Standing Up** *(the Table, by secure video)*
The Table meets about the blog, the store and the succession. Seat Eleven (Commons), the Gardener who wrote down Quill's words, has just learned that their own child is **not on the list**. They say Marguerite's line, *"a gardener still chooses the rows"*, and leave the call. It's **the first Seat lost in thirty-nine years.** Seat Four orders that the Wharf Road vote go Larkspire's way, whatever it costs.

**28. Wharf Road** *(late September)*
The council meeting on the redevelopment. Clearfell and Frostline both turn up, primed to fight each other. The Mission Hall network turns up too: tenants, the new co-op, a community land trust bid. Danny keeps the room. **Jack** speaks, as both Jordan and Jack: the feeds' name and the paperwork's. The vote is deferred, not won. *Ends:* every Larkspire tenant on the estate receives the same automated text: *"Routine inspection of your home scheduled. No action required."* Jack reads it aloud. Someone says: *"What if nobody opens the door?"*

**29. Nobody Opens the Door** *(early October)*
**The rent strike.** It starts with forty flats on Wharf Road refusing Larkspire's "routine inspection" and withholding rent together, organised at the Mission Hall. People who stood on opposite sides of the Wall sit at one table and sign the same letter. Nell posts it, with no names and no villains, just the method. Larkspire tenants elsewhere copy it: other cities, then other countries, wherever Larkspire funds own homes. Within days, tenants on four continents are refusing the same inspection on the same day. *Missing knock (D14):* the strike network sets up its own knock rota: neighbours checking on neighbours, while every door stays shut to the inspector. Cobb's ending gains weight: he's the one who knocked, and now he signs. *The line (D15):* on his night, at 02:10, the phone box is lit and its phone **rings**. Nobody answers. It rings until it stops. The next day she finds his name on the list.
- **What it is:** lawful, collective and non-violent. Tenants unite and stop paying the rent. Clearfell's and Frostline's recruiters try to turn it into a fight with each other and fail, because the people they're working on are now sitting at the same table.
- **What it does:** the Table's money stalls. Factors panic. Seat Four can't find anyone to blame, because every decoy is in the room.
- **The hall that Thursday:** too many people for the chairs, so they fetch more. Ama, Danny, Jack, Teo, Len (back for the first time since his coffee), Graham from across the road. Ruth at the back. Crane by the door, near the exit and choosing not to use it. They go round the room and each person says one sentence beginning "I".
- **Nell's last blog post:** what she knows, what she doesn't, the method, and *"I'm not asking who's to blame."*
- **Cobb's ending:** on the strike list from another estate, Nell finds the name of the polite man who used to read her day back to her. He's a Larkspire tenant too. He signed. He never speaks to her again, and he never needs to.
- **All invented:** Larkspire is fictional, and no real company, real protest or real unrest is named or mirrored (canon decision D13).

**30. Interesting** *(Wednesday 14 October 2026, Day 23,376)*
Sixty-four years to the night. Mother Quill dies at her farm at 02:09. At 02:10 in the Ashby store, which Teo is watching on a borrowed camera, the teletype begins to type. It isn't to the Table. It isn't to anyone. It types one word, the first in six decades not written as counsel, and then the three tones, softer than before:

> *INTERESTING.*

**What it means, on two rungs at once:** the Tenants have watched tenants on every rung of the ladder refuse an inspection together, something that isn't on any inventory and can't be harvested. Either it threatens the lease, or it's the first thing on this farm worth keeping. And perhaps they're wondering whether tenants can refuse *their* landlord too. The cull is **postponed, not cancelled**.

Nell reads it on Teo's phone at her kitchen table, in front of a wall with a crossed chair above six cards. On the final page, without naming any real event, she reflects that **every age has had its peasants' revolt**: the people at the bottom of the ladder have always eventually looked sideways, and that's why the ledger has never been finished. The book's thesis lands in her voice: *the only thing the ladder can't hold is people choosing each other.* She takes out a cheap ruled notebook and, at the top of a clean page, writes the date the way her side of the field will count it from now on. *(Last line to be found in drafting. It must answer chapter 1's* Day 0 *without copying it.)*

**Epilogue: Notice** *(November 2026, about 1,000 words or less; Nell, first person)*
A few weeks later. The rent strike is holding. Mother Quill is buried. The hall has a rota. Life is smaller and better. Then, at ten past two one morning, her phone rings. For about a second, before Teo speaks, there's the hiss with the rhythm of speech: the line Cobb reported into (D15). She never mentions it. Then Teo: the Ashby teletype is printing, and it isn't counsel. It's a **notice**, formal, in a typeface none of them has seen, addressed *TO THE TENANTS OF THIS HOUSE*. An inspection has been scheduled. The date will follow. Nobody wrote it. Not the Table, not the Tenants. It came *through* them, the way post comes through an agent's office. The machine is silent for an hour. Then, beneath the notice, slowly, it types one character, and stops:

> *I*

Nell looks at it for a long time. The book ends on her first thought, one line, still to be found in drafting. It must be about choosing sides, not fear.
- **Launch-day ARG drop:** the notice itself, released online as an in-world artefact with its date redacted, starting Book 2's game. Fiction trail included; no real date or countdown (rules 4–7). See the [campaign plan](../campaign/campaign-plan.md).
- **Hooks into Book 2:** the Freeholder as the threat; who said "I"; the inspection date; Nell offered the chair; who rang her at 02:10 (D15).

## Length

- **Target: a full-length novel of 70,000–80,000 words** (Martin's ruling, 26 Oct 2026). That's about 2,300–2,700 words per chapter.
- **Act budgets:** Act One ~22,000–24,000 · Act Two ~26,000–30,000 · Act Three ~22,000–26,000.
- **Interludes** ~1,400–2,000 words. **Key chapters** (1, the Wall, 26, 30) ~3,000–5,000.
- **Grow by expanding Act Two, not by adding plot.**
- Chapters 1–5 already fit this pace; none needs changing for length alone.

## Rules check

- Every company, group and person is fictional. Real history appears only as backdrop (the October 1962 crisis, unnamed).
- Every flashpoint is invented (the Ferris Library, Wharf Road, the rumour).
- No real movement or group is shown as an instrument of the plan or as fake.
- Danny's words in chapter 17 are never quoted.
- Clearfell and Frostline get no slogans, symbols or recruitment copy that could be lifted.
- The ending is organising and community, not confrontation. The Table is surprised, not destroyed.
- **The rent strike is lawful, non-violent and invented.** No real riots or uprisings are named or used as models (canon decision D13). "Every age's peasants' revolt" is alluded to only in general terms.
- **The cull and the Taken** appear only in the book, with method never shown and selection never by group (canon decisions D11, D12). The deep-time reveal credits humans with everything they built (D13).

### Book 2 setting (Martin, 28 Oct 2026; reference only)

- **When:** about ten years after Book 1, so around 2036.
- **Where:** America. Civil war has broken out on the East and West coasts. Vesper (Nell) has moved to **Washington, DC**, the capital, between the two fronts (Martin, 28 Oct 2026).
- **Guardrails (hard rules 1, 2, 4, 5 and 9):**
  - The war is **invented**. Its sides, leaders, militias and flashpoints are fictional composites, never mapped onto real US parties, movements, states' politics or real people.
  - The twist holds: the Lodge feeds **every side at once**, and no real group is ever the war's cause or instrument.
  - No real US event (an election, attack, riot or disaster) is retold or blamed on the Lodge or the Tenants.
  - It's **book only** until decided otherwise. No ARG content about an American civil war goes into real US political conversations, hashtags or communities.
  - Book 1's setting rule (no real present-day towns or institutions) would have to change for Book 2 to name Washington (open question 17).

## The teaser: why epilogue plus ARG drop

- **Impact (in the book):** chapter 30 ends cleanly on *INTERESTING*. The **epilogue**, a few weeks later, delivers two shocks. First, a notice the machine didn't write, addressed *TO THE TENANTS OF THIS HOUSE*, announcing an inspection. Then, for the first time in history, the machine types **I**.
- **Staying power (outside the book):** on launch day, **the notice** is released online as a new in-world artefact, with its date redacted. Readers who finish the book find the story still moving, and that's the start of Book 2's waiting list.
- **Safety:** the drop carries the fiction trail. There's no real date or countdown, and nothing that reads as a real-world prophecy (rules 4–7).

# Act 2 scene: "The Wall"

## Purpose

The **midpoint** of the novel, where the central twist lands for the reader. Up to here, Vesper has been collecting *evidence of a plan*. In this scene she and Danny see the *shape* of it. Every pressure in their lives turns neighbours towards each other in suspicion, and none points up at the people doing it. The Lodge is missing from its own map, and that absence is the design.

By the end, the story's question changes from *"Is this real?"* to *"What do we do about it, together?"*

## Where we are in the story

- Vesper has read the full Harrowgate Minutes (Act 2 opening) and knows the counsel lines.
- Crane is still secretly her handler (canon decision D6). He's been helpful, and that's starting to feel too convenient.
- Two days earlier, **the rumour** (invented flashpoint, [modern pressures §6](../../bible/modern-pressures.md)) nearly turned into a street confrontation outside the Mission Hall. Nobody was hurt. Danny was in the middle of it, and he's still shaking.
- Vesper doesn't yet know Larkspire owns her building. **That reveal happens in this scene.**

## Setting

Vesper's flat, second floor, about 1 a.m. One wall is still covered with Phase 1–2 material: manifests, day-counts, the card 0144 printout, string. Rain outside. Across the road, Graham's grey door.

---

## Beats

### 1. Clearing the wall (≈400 words)
- Vesper can't sleep. She takes down weeks of manifests and dates. *"I'd been reconciling the past. I wanted to reconcile now."*
- **Image:** the clean wall, with drawing-pin holes like a star map.
- She takes a pack of **5×3 index cards** from a drawer. It's a deliberate echo of the Harlow-Vane card index. She notices the echo and does it anyway.

### 2. Danny at the door (≈500 words)
- He knocks at 1:20 a.m., still in his delivery jacket. He hasn't slept since the rumour.
- He describes the confrontation in fragments: who shouted, who he recognised on both sides, the moment he heard himself saying something he'd have been ashamed of a year ago. **He doesn't repeat it.**
- Vesper doesn't absolve him. She hands him a tea and a marker pen.

### 3. Six cards (≈900 words)
Vesper writes one pressure per card, in capitals, and pins them in a row. Under each card she pins the matching **Minutes counsel**, copied in the Tenants' typeface:

| Card | Counsel pinned beneath |
|---|---|
| **THE RACE** | *LET THEM RACE. NO ONE WHO IS RUNNING LOOKS AT THE ROAD.* |
| **OWN NOTHING** | *LET THEM RENT THE GROUND THEY STAND ON. A TENANT DOES NOT PLANT TREES.* |
| **WATCHED** | *(none found yet. Vesper leaves a blank square)* |
| **THE HUSH** | *LET EACH SIDE FEAR THE OTHERS MOUTH. THEN NEITHER WILL OPEN ITS OWN.* |
| **BELONGING** | *GIVE EVERY FEAR A FACE THAT IS NOT OURS.* |
| **OUTRAGE** | *A FIELD THAT IS CROWDED FIGHTS ITSELF. LET IT.* |

Then the **fictional evidence**, one thread per card, in Vesper's analyst shorthand:
- **Race:** the Coyle's routing server, Day 22,861, Teo's DC-3 power cap.
- **Own nothing:** the Wharf Road scheme's developer.
- **Watched:** the keycard logs (canon decision D5); Cobb reciting her day.
- **Hush:** the Ferris Library threats, which came from accounts on *both* sides.
- **Belonging:** the rumour, with different versions in different feeds.
- **Outrage:** @longweather_, and the Clearfell and Frostline recruiters.

**Danny's scepticism, kept alive:** "Half of this is just how the world is, Nell. Rents, cameras, people being arseholes online. You don't need aliens." Vesper: "I know. That's what's frightening. They don't need to *do* much."

### 4. The names (≈900 words). The turn.
- Danny takes a stack of blank cards and says: "You've got the what. I'll give you the who."
- He writes **neighbours' names**, all characters established earlier in the book, under each pressure. He puts them on **two sides of each card**: the people it's hurting on the left, and the people being *blamed* for it on the right.
- **The pattern emerges:**
  - **The same names keep crossing sides.** The woman blamed for "taking" a flat on Wharf Road is the one priced out of her own. The lad blamed in the rumour, Jordan Pryce, is the apprentice who lost his place to the routing system. The man who complained about the Ferris talk had his own name on the threat list.
  - **Every piece of string runs sideways, neighbour to neighbour. Nothing runs up.**
- Danny says it before she does: **"Where's their name?"**
- **There's nowhere to pin one.** There isn't a single card for the Lodge, for Larkspire's directors, for anyone at the Table. The map of everything hurting the street has no room for the people causing it.
- Vesper (narration): *"It wasn't that they'd hidden themselves on the wall. It was that the wall was built so there was nowhere for them to go."*

### 5. The seventh card (≈400 words)
- Vesper takes one more card and draws the **chair with a line through it**, the Archivist's sign. She pins it **above** the row, in empty space, with no string attached.
- Danny: "That's not much of an answer." Vesper: "It's a direction."
- **Callback:** canon decision D1. Twelve chairs, one empty. *"Thirteen chairs. Never more."* For the first time, Vesper understands that Marguerite was counting the guest.

### 6. Confessions (≈700 words)
- **Danny** finally repeats, quietly, what he said at the confrontation. *(Off the page: the reader is told he said it and how it felt, not the words themselves.)* He says he believed it at the time. He says he still has the fear underneath it, even now he knows the fear was being *used*. Vesper doesn't tell him the fear was fake. The pressures are real, but the target was wrong.
- **Vesper** tells Danny about card 0212 and her father: *"Reliable. Asks no questions."* She's afraid she's her father's daughter in the wrong way: someone who keeps the paperwork tidy for people she never sees.
- **The missing knock (D14).** Below the six cards, Nell has pinned the three deaths and her own people: Danny, Ama, Jack, Crane, Cobb, and her own post. She maps who *didn't* knock, and every arrow runs sideways, at a neighbour. Danny explains the parcels (the app's ninety-second door timer, ch 5). Ama's reason for the key comes by text or in Danny's telling: the old man asked for it back because the feeds had made him afraid of visitors. Vesper sees she has done what the letter warned against. *(Estimate: about +300 words, which puts this section at about 1,000.)*
- Neither consoles the other well. That's the point. They stay in the room anyway.

### 7. The building (≈500 words). The Larkspire reveal.
- At 3:40 a.m., Vesper's phone lights up with an automated message from the building's managing agent: **"Larkspire Property Services: routine inspection of your flat scheduled for 10:00 today. No action required."**
- She's never heard the name attached to her building before. She goes still. Danny reads it over her shoulder and says nothing for a long time.
- She takes a blank card, writes **LARKSPIRE**, and looks for where to pin it. It goes under **OWN NOTHING**, and under **WATCHED**, because the inspection is the day after the wall goes up.
- She rereads the counsel under **OWN NOTHING**: *"A TENANT DOES NOT PLANT TREES."* She'd read "tenant" as a pun on the Tenants. Now she realises it means *her*.
- The **WATCHED** card still has no counsel. Vesper pencils in the blank square: *"They don't need to say it. I can feel it."*

### 8. Not posting it (≈300 words)
- Vesper photographs the wall for the blog, then stops. Her neighbours' names are on it.
- She deletes the photo. *"This is exactly what they'd want me to post."* It's a direct link to the Hush: the urge to broadcast the pattern, with names, would feed the same machine.
- **Instead** she writes one line in her notebook for the next blog post: *"Every arrow on my wall points sideways."* (A future blog beat. See the production notes.)

### 9. Closing image (≈300 words)
- Dawn. Danny asleep on the sofa, still in his jacket.
- Vesper looks across at Graham's grey door, then at the empty space above the six cards.
- She texts **Pastor Ama**: *"Is the hall free on Thursday? I need to get some people round a table."*
- **Last line (draft):** *"Twelve chairs and one empty. I'd need more than that."*

## Writing notes

- **Voice:** Vesper's first person, dry and exact, doubting herself (see [voices](../../bible/voices.md)). This is the most emotional chapter in Act 2, so keep the humour in to stop it tipping into speech-making.
- **Show, don't lecture.** The pattern comes from cards, names and string, not from anyone explaining the theme. Nobody says "every scapegoat is a decoy" aloud. The reader gets there half a beat before Danny does.
- **Danny's line in Beat 4 should be the single most quoted line of the book.** Keep it short.
- **Physical detail anchors the abstract:** drawing pins, the marker squeaking, the tea going cold, the 3:40 a.m. phone glow.

## Rule checks (CLAUDE.md, modern pressures)

- **Only fictional people and organisations** appear on the wall: neighbours, Larkspire, Coyle's, DC-3, the invented flashpoints. No real movements, groups, companies or people.
- **Danny's words at the confrontation are never quoted.** We show the effect, never the scapegoating line itself, so nothing lifted from the book reads as an endorsement.
- **Group identities are deliberately unspecified** on the "blamed" side of the cards. Readers can't map them onto any one real group, which is the point: it's whoever each feed was told to fear.
- **The pressures stay real; the targets are false.** The scene never dismisses rents, cameras or job losses as imaginary.
- **No real-world call to action.** The scene ends with organising a community meeting, not confronting anyone.
