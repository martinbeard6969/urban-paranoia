## Phase 4: Reveal (W16–W20)

**Goal:** expose the twist publicly and launch the book.

- Vesper exposes **@longweather_ as a Lodge plant**. The decoy is shown to be a deliberate tool.
- The story's message lands: *every scapegoat is a decoy*.
- The ARG resolves into the novel's first act. All channels gain a visible "Urban Paranoia, a novel" banner.
- **Book launch.**
- **Launch-day drop:** the Freeholder's **notice**, addressed *TO THE TENANTS OF THIS HOUSE*, released online as a new in-world artefact with the date redacted. It mirrors the book's epilogue and opens Book 2's game. Fiction trail on the page; no real date, countdown or prophecy (rules 4–7).

## Phase 5: Expansion (post-launch)

- Teasers for the film and comics are seeded as new "evidence".
- Comics anthology: one Seat per issue.
- Channels stay up as clearly labelled fiction.

## News desk (all phases)

From launch, a weekly [news desk](news-desk.md) digest proposes real trends to use as backdrop (Vesper's "Weather reports", fictional echoes, plot seeds). Every use is rated, held for a cooling-off period and signed off by an editor.

## Success measures

- Puzzle solve times. A puzzle should take between ~6 hours and ~3 days to solve. Faster than that means it's too easy; slower means it needs a nudge.
- Size and activity of the community (comments, forum or Discord threads).
- Clicks from `/about` pages through to pre-orders.
- **Safety measure:** number of people who take the story as real-world fact and act on it. The target is zero. See the [moderation & safety playbook](moderation-and-safety.md).

# Moderation & safety playbook

The campaign deliberately blurs the line between fiction and reality, and it deals with themes (economic insecurity, distrust of elites, hidden plans) that real conspiracy movements use. This playbook keeps that blur **inside the story**. Everyone who posts in character must read it.

## Principles

1. **The ambiguity is an atmosphere, not a trap.** Anyone who asks sincerely whether this is real gets a truthful path to the answer.
2. **People come before immersion.** When immersion conflicts with someone's wellbeing or safety, break character.
3. **The story condemns scapegoating, so moderation must too.** We never let our channels host real-world blame of real groups, even "in character".
4. **Nothing leaves the screen.** No real-world locations, meet-ups, deliveries or confrontations unless they're organised, ticketed events that are openly part of the campaign.

## Comment moderation (Vesper's blog and campaign channels)

| Comment type | Action |
|---|---|
| Theories, puzzle-solving, speculation about **fictional** entities | Allow. Encourage. |
| Scepticism, "this is an ARG", "this is marketing" | Allow. Don't delete or argue. Vesper can reply lightly in character ("D. says the same"). |
| Blaming a **real** ethnic, religious, national or migrant group, or a real political movement | **Delete.** Don't reply in character with arguments. Repeat offenders are banned. Log it. |
| Naming **real people** as Lodge members or Stewards | **Delete.** Log it. |
| Real addresses, phone numbers, or anyone's personal details (including guesses at the "real" Vesper or Crane) | **Delete at once.** Ban. Escalate if it looks like doxxing. |
| Plans to visit, watch or confront real places or people | **Delete.** Post an out-of-character note (see below). Escalate. |
| Health, financial or safety claims (e.g. "the plan is in the vaccines", "pull your money out") | **Delete.** Out-of-character note if it's spreading. |
| Distress, or apparent belief that the person is personally being watched or targeted | **Break character privately** (see below). |
| Abuse, harassment, threats | Delete, ban, escalate. |

### Out-of-character note (public)
Post it from a clearly labelled account (e.g. "Urban Paranoia team"), never from Vesper:

> Quick note from the team behind this story: *Urban Paranoia* is a work of fiction. Please don't go looking for any real places or people mentioned in the comments. Everything in this story lives online. [About the project](/about)

## Book-only themes in player spaces

The cull, the Taken, deep time and the rent strike belong to the book and film. If players bring them into the game:

| Player content | Action |
|---|---|
| Linking "the cull" or "the thinning" to real vaccines, medicine, food, technology, companies or events | **Delete.** Post the out-of-character note. Escalate if it spreads. |
| Claiming real people or groups are "Taken", "chosen" or "being thinned" | **Delete.** Ban repeat offenders. |
| Crediting real ancient monuments or cultures to aliens ("ancient aliens") | **Delete.** Brief out-of-character note: in this story, humans built everything. |
| Linking the story to real riots or unrest, or calling for real-world violence or confrontation | **Delete at once.** Escalate. |
| Discussing the fictional Larkspire rent strike as story | Allow. |
| Asking about real tenants' rights | Allow, and point to official tenant-advice services. Never give legal advice in character. |

## Breaking character with an individual

**Triggers:** distress, fear for their own safety, signs of paranoia that seem to go beyond enjoying the game, signs they're a minor, or a direct and sincere "please tell me if this is real".

**How:** a private message from the team account, not from a character:

> Hi, this is the team behind *Urban Paranoia*, the story Vesper's blog is part of. It's fiction: Vesper, the Lodge and the Tenants are all invented. We wanted to make sure you knew that, and we're glad you're enjoying it. If any of it has been weighing on you, it's completely fine to step away. [About the project](/about)

Add local support resources if the person seems to be in real distress. Don't push them to keep playing.

## @longweather_ (special handling)

@longweather_ is the antagonist account that spreads decoy blame. **It's the most dangerous asset in the campaign**, because done badly, it *is* real-world scapegoating.

- **Default: it exists only in the story.** It's described in Vesper's posts and seen as deleted or summarised comments on her blog. We never write its blame content in full, and it never names a real group.
- **It must not be created as a real account on any social platform** without:
  - a written plan for what it posts, reviewed against the hard rules
  - legal and platform-policy sign-off
  - its content being limited to *fictional* targets only (e.g. it blames "the Harlow-Vane people" or "the Ashby lot", never real groups)
- If real users start quoting or copying the account's style to blame real groups, handle it as a scapegoating comment: delete, don't engage, and log it. Bring the Phase 4 reveal forward if it becomes a pattern.

## In-world inboxes (e.g. Crane's `archive@`)

- Only open one if someone is staffing it, with replies within 48 hours.
- Every reply follows the rules in this playbook and the hard rules. Vulnerable or distressed correspondents get the break-character message.
- Every reply's signature includes a small "a story by…" line linking to `/about`.
- Never ask correspondents for personal details. Never send anything physical to a player's address.

## Press and enquiries

- If a journalist asks whether this is real, **confirm it's a fiction campaign for the book**, off the record if necessary. Don't lie to the press.
- If a regulator, platform or law-enforcement body asks, disclose fully and at once.

## Escalation

| Severity | Examples | Who | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | Repeated off-topic or scapegoating comments | Moderator | Same day |
| Medium | Doxxing attempts, confrontation plans, distressed players | Moderator → named publisher contact | Within 2 hours |
| High | Threats, real-world incidents, press reporting it as real | Publisher contact → legal | Immediately. Pause new content until resolved. |

## Incident log

Keep a simple log (date, channel, what happened, action taken). Review it weekly during the campaign and before each phase starts.

# For the Long Weather

*Posted by Vesper*

I know how this sounds. I'm writing it down anyway. Writing things down is the one skill I have left that a machine hasn't been paid to do better.

---

For eleven years I planned freight routes for Coyle Brothers Haulage. You won't have heard of them, and that's the point. Coyle's is the firm behind the firm behind the shelf where your shopping was before you bought it. I knew which depot rounded its diesel up and which driver's tyre always went flat the Friday before a bank holiday. My old manager called me a human spreadsheet with opinions. I took it as a compliment. I still do.

Last May they switched on a routing system that does what I did, faster, without opinions. To be fair, it's better at it. Our team of nine became a team of one, and the one is a contractor who "supervises the outputs" from a laptop several time zones away. I received a fair settlement and a letter thanking me for my *contribution to the transition*. I have read that letter more often than anything else I own, including my tenancy agreement, which is at least honest about who's in charge.

So I'm not neutral. Keep that in mind as you read.

---

## The box

Part of my notice was spent in the archive: a storage unit by the old rail yard, eighty years of paper in cardboard boxes gone soft at the corners. Someone had to decide what to scan and what to shred, and that someone was the person they were already letting go.

Manifests are the dullest documents people make. That's their job. Weight, rate, origin, destination, initials. After a few thousand of them your eyes stop reading and start *sorting*, the way your hand knows the bottom stair in the dark. Paper from the same year has the same texture, the same typeface, the same tired clerks. You stop seeing the documents. You only see what doesn't fit.

In a box marked **1971 Q1 — MISC.**, something didn't fit.

It's a single sheet on a standard Coyle's form of the period. It records one consignment, collected from **Harrowgate House** and delivered to **The Ashby Trust**, care of a PO box. The cargo line reads:

> OFFICE EQUIPMENT — TELETYPE APPARATUS, 1 UNIT. HANDLE AS INSTRUCTED.

People moved teletypes in 1971. That isn't strange. This is:

1. **There's no weight.** Every Coyle's manifest has a weight, because weight is what we billed by. This one has a neat pencil line through the box, the way you'd cross out a question you'd been told not to ask.
2. **There's no charge.** No rate, no invoice number. Coyle's has never moved anything for free, not in 1971 and not last year.
3. **The date isn't a date.** Where the collection date belongs, someone has written **D. 3062**. I took it for a docket number. I checked the dockets. It isn't one.
4. **And across the bottom**, in purple from a rubber stamp, slightly crooked, as if pressed by someone who'd done it many times and stopped looking:

> **FOR THE LONG WEATHER**

No company name. No logo. Just those four words, in a colour you'd use for a wedding invitation.

---

## What I've already checked, so you don't have to tell me

- **Could it be a joke?** It could. But it was in a sealed box, under forty other sealed boxes, on the same paper and in the same ink as everything around it. If it's a prank, it's been waiting fifty-five years for an audience.
- **Is "the long weather" a shipping term?** Not one I know. I asked two retired drivers who know everything. One laughed. The other went quiet, said he'd seen it "on the odd job", and asked about my mum. I'm not reading anything into that. (I'm reading something into that.)
- **What is Harrowgate House?** As far as I can tell, nowhere. The postcode fragment on the sheet matches nothing that exists today. Places don't usually vanish. This one seems to have been tidied away.
- **What was the Ashby Trust?** A charity, dissolved years ago. Its registered purpose was *the study of long-term social conditions*. That's everything I have, and it's already more than I'd like.

---

## Why I'm telling you

Partly because I have time. A sheet of paper that doesn't add up is the most interesting thing that's happened to me since the pastries at my redundancy briefing.

But mostly because of how it *feels*. I've handled maybe two hundred thousand manifests. They're designed to be forgotten. This one was designed to *look* forgettable, which isn't the same thing. Someone left out the weight, the charge and the date, and kept the stamp. That isn't carelessness. That's a habit, and habits belong to people who expect to do the same thing again.

I'll say one more thing, once, and then try not to say it again. The people who wrote my job out of existence talk a great deal about *the long term*. I know that isn't evidence. I know it's a redundant woman finding her own reflection in a filing box.

So I'm going to do the only thing I was ever properly trained for. I'm going to reconcile it. The missing weight, the missing charge, the date that isn't a date. I'll post what I find, including the parts where I'm wrong, which I expect to be most of them.

If you've ever seen that stamp, or those four words, on anything at all, tell me. If you think I've lost my mind, tell me that too. I'd rather be told.

More soon.

— V.

# Day Zero

*Posted by Vesper*

I expected four readers and my mum. Instead there were three hundred comments, eleven emails, and one stranger who kindly offered to have me sectioned. Thank you, all of you, him included. It's been a long time since anyone argued with me about something I'd found.

Some housekeeping, because many of you asked the same things:

- **Yes, I still have the manifest.** It lives in a plastic sleeve in my kitchen drawer, between the takeaway menus and the spare fuse. A better scan is at the bottom of this post.
- **No, I won't say where the storage unit is.** It still belongs to Coyle's, and I'd like to stay on polite terms with their lawyers.
- **Several of you guessed that "D. 3062" was a count of days.** You were right, and I'm annoyed I didn't see it first. Let me at least show my working.

---

## Reconciling the date

A manifest without a date is useless to a haulier, so Coyle's kept a second record that nobody ever thought to fake: **vehicle logs**. Every lorry, every day: the driver, mileage out, mileage in, a one-line job reference. It's the sort of paperwork that only lies about small things.

The 1971 logs were three shelves below the box from Post #1. I read the first quarter line by line over two evenings, with a flask of coffee and a back I'll be paying for until summer.

On **Wednesday 3 March 1971**, a driver logged a round trip with no job reference. Where the reference should be, in a careful hand, he'd written **"H.H. — per instr."** The mileage puts the destination about two and a half hours away, each way, somewhere rural.

Harrowgate House.

So if that day was 3,062, then counting began 3,062 days earlier. I did the sum by hand, then in a spreadsheet, then by hand again, because I no longer trust anything I can't check twice:

> **Day 0 = 14 October 1962.**

Some of you will know why that date makes people uneasy. I'm not going to write about it. I don't want this to become *that* kind of blog. But it was not a quiet week for the world to start counting.

---

## The 1962 box

If something started in October 1962, and Coyle's was there in 1971, I wondered whether Coyle's had been there at the beginning.

The box marked **1962 Q4** had been in the damp corner. Most of it has fused into a single block of paper, like geology. But one manifest came away whole.

**Saturday 13 October 1962.** A delivery *to* Harrowgate House. Sender: **"Harlow-Vane Inst., per C.S.S. dept."** Cargo:

> OFFICE EQUIPMENT — TELETYPE APPARATUS, 1 UNIT, WITH LINE CONNECTION.

This one is properly ordinary. It has a rate, an invoice number, a signature. The weight was there once, but the corner where it was written has gone soft and brown. There's no stamp.

Beneath it, in the driver's hand:

> *Delivered & installed. Gent at door said would be "needed from tomorrow." Odd sort of party — 7 cars on the drive, no staff.*

I've read that line more times than my redundancy letter. *Needed from tomorrow.* Seven cars and nobody to park them. A teletype carried into that house on a Saturday, the day before the counting started. Eight years and four months later, perhaps the same machine came back out, unweighed, unbilled and stamped.

As far as I can tell, "Harlow-Vane Inst." is the **Harlow-Vane Institute for Systems Studies**, which sounds as dull as it was presumably meant to. It closed years ago. I'll come back to it.

---

## Len

In Post #1 I mentioned two retired drivers. One laughed, and one went quiet. I'll call the quiet one Len, which is nothing like his name.

I took Len a coffee and the new scan. He held it at arm's length the way people do with photographs of the dead. This is as close to his words as I can manage:

> "Twice a year, maybe. Never on the board. The office'd ring you at home the night before. No paperwork in the cab. You'd collect, you'd deliver, and a fella'd meet you at the far end and pay you separate. Cash. Polite. Good suit. You'd not remember his face after. Not a bad face. Just one that didn't stick.
>
> "The stamp was on the office copy, not yours. You only saw it if you went looking. I went looking the once."

I asked what he'd carried. "Machines, mostly." Teletypes, then "them fax things", and once something the size of a wardrobe that "hummed with the power off". He said that last part, heard himself say it, and looked at his coffee as if it had been talking.

I asked why he'd gone quiet when I first mentioned the stamp.

> "Because my mate Terry asked, back in the eighties. Asked the office who the cash man was. And then Terry wasn't on long-distance any more. Nothing nasty. He just never got the good runs again. You notice that sort of thing. You notice it for forty years."

Nobody vanished. Nobody died. A man lost his good shifts for asking a question. That happens at every firm in the country, and I know it. It's the *forty years* I can't put down.

---

## The sensible voice

I have a friend I'll call D. He was a union rep for twenty years, until there was no union left to represent, and now he delivers parcels for an app that rates him out of five. D. left a comment under Post #1, which some of you saw before I could do anything about it: *"It's a tax dodge, Nell."*

(Hello. Now you know my first name. Thank you, D.)

His theory is that a rich charity moved its kit off the books to keep it out of the accounts: the cash, the missing weights, the funny stamp, all of it just rich people's bookkeeping. It's a good theory. It explains nearly everything. He's usually right, and he knows it. Then he said something else, and I want it on the record:

> "Whatever this is, it'll be some bloke in a suit. It always is. Don't let anyone tell you it's the people down your street."

I'll come back to why he said that.

---

## About the comments

# Count Forwards

*Posted by Nell (Vesper)*

I've started this post four times. The first three were angry, and anger makes for bad paperwork. So I'll do what I was trained to do and write it as an incident report: what happened, in order, and as little as possible about how it felt.

---

## 1. A line I didn't write

The morning after Post #2 went up, a reader emailed to ask what I'd meant by the note hidden in the page's source code:

> *3062 days out. 22,8xx days in. still counting.*

I hadn't meant anything by it. I hadn't written it. Until that email I didn't know a page *had* a source you could hide things in. I had to look it up to understand the question.

The site keeps a history of every edit. The line was added at **03:12 on the night Post #2 went live**, by someone logged in with my username and my password.

I changed the password. I switched on every security setting the platform offers, including two I'm still not sure I understand. D. said I should report it, and I have. I told him it was probably a bored reader with a password-guessing program. He gave me the look he used to give managers who said the pension scheme was *fine*.

---

## 2. The letter

Nine days later an envelope came to my flat. My **home address** has never appeared on this blog, or on anything of mine I can find.

It was a plain envelope with a second-class stamp and a smudged postmark. Inside was one sheet, typed on a real typewriter: under the kitchen light you can feel where each key bit into the paper. Across the top runs a thin band of grey, the colour of a sky that hasn't decided to rain. There's no other letterhead.

Here it is. I've read it perhaps a hundred times, and I'd like someone to share the weight.

> MS [—].
>
> YOU COUNTED BACKWARDS. GOOD. NOW COUNT FORWARDS.
>
> 3062 DAYS OUT. THAT WAS THE MACHINE LEAVING HARROWGATE.
>
> 22861 DAYS IN. THAT WAS THE MACHINE ARRIVING AT YOUR DESK.
>
> THE WEIGHT WAS ALWAYS THE SAME. SEE ENCLOSED.
>
> I AM SORRY ABOUT YOUR WEBSITE. YOUR PASSWORD WAS YOUR FATHERS BIRTHDAY. YOU SHOULD CHANGE IT. YOU SHOULD ALSO ASK WHY I KNOW IT.
>
> DO NOT BLAME ANYONE YOU CAN SEE FROM YOUR WINDOW. THAT IS WHAT THEY WANT.
>
> WHEN YOU ARE READY THE DOOR IS GREY.

It used my full name. I've taken that out. You can have the rest.

There's no signature. At the foot of the page is a small ink drawing of a chair, the plain wooden kind you'd find round a kitchen table, with one clean line struck through it.

And yes, my password was my father's birthday. I know. He'd have been disappointed in me, though not surprised.

---

## 3. Counting forwards

Day 0 is 14 October 1962. Count forwards 22,861 days and you arrive at:

> **Saturday 17 May 2025.**

I didn't need to look it up. I looked it up anyway.

That was the day Coyle's switched on the routing system, the day a team of nine was told it would be a team of one. I remember it because it was a Saturday and they'd called us in for a "transition briefing" with pastries. Almond croissants. Nobody ate them. They sat on the table through the whole presentation, like the last guests at a party nobody wanted to leave first.

*The machine arriving at your desk.*

So the letter puts a teletype leaving Harrowgate and the system that replaced me on one continuous tally, fifty-four years apart, as if they were two entries in the same ledger. I don't know what to do with that. I'm trying very hard not to do anything with it yet.

---

## 4. The enclosures

There were two photocopies in the envelope.

**The first** is another copy of the 1971 manifest. Not mine: mine, I'm now fairly sure, is the office copy Len told me about. This one travelled with the machine and was signed for at the far end: *recd. Ashby Tst.* It's the same consignment, with the same purple stamp, pressed harder. But on this copy, the weight box is filled in:

> **144 LB.**

**The second** is a Coyle's **goods-in note from May 2025**, for the server cabinet installed to run the routing system. It's the grey box in the comms room that we used to joke was our replacement, back when it was a joke. Weight:

> **144 LB.**

I asked D. about this, because being the sensible voice is his job, and he did it:

> "That's about what a small rack cabinet weighs, Nell. Old teletypes weren't light either. Heavy office kit is heavy office kit."

He's right. A hundred and forty-four pounds is a common weight for a heavy box. A coincidence is nothing.

But someone found both documents, fifty-four years apart. Someone copied them, folded them into a plain envelope and posted them to an address I've never given anyone, to make sure I'd notice. The coincidence may be nothing. The *noticing* was arranged.

---

## 5. Dad

The letter tells me to ask why the writer knows my father's birthday. So, in the spirit of reconciling everything:

My father worked in Coyle's **head office** for thirty years, from 1979 until he retired. He was a clerk, then a senior clerk, then "the man who knew where everything was". It's how I got my first interview there. He died in 2019. He was kind and quiet, finished the cryptic crossword in pen, and as far as I ever knew he never kept a secret in his life.

I'm not going to speculate about him in public. I'm putting down the facts because I promised to post everything, especially the parts I'd rather not.

Len said the office would ring the drivers at home the night before one of those runs.

I'm going to stop this paragraph here.

---

## 6. What I think, for what it's worth

I think one person sent this. They're patient and precise, and they've been carrying something heavy for a long time. The chair doesn't feel like a logo. It feels like a signature someone gave up their name for. And the line I keep coming back to has nothing to do with teletypes or weights:

> DO NOT BLAME ANYONE YOU CAN SEE FROM YOUR WINDOW. THAT IS WHAT THEY WANT.

Whoever this is, they've just said what D. said in my kitchen two weeks ago, almost word for word. I didn't expect a stranger and my oldest friend to agree about anything.

I also think I might be being played: by a prankster, by someone who's been watching me, or by someone with a reason I can't see yet. I'm holding both possibilities open. I'd ask you to hold them too.

More soon. I'm going to go and change every password I've ever had, including the ones I'd forgotten.

# The Harlow-Vane Institute for Systems Studies: holding site

## Concept

A dormant archive website for a research institute that closed in 2009. It looks built in 2011 and abandoned in 2017. It's earnest, a little vain, and almost deserted.

**The craft here is controlled dullness.** Every public page should read like something a real retired academic would maintain out of loyalty. Nothing on any single page is sinister. The dread builds up across them: in the gaps, in a publications list that reads like a plan once you've seen the plan, and in a card index that remembers everyone.

**In-world, the site is kept going by one volunteer: Dr. Hollis Crane, Honorary Archivist.** That's how he knows when card 0144 has been opened, and why he writes to Vesper.

# The Card Index

*Posted by Nell (Vesper)*

It took you nineteen hours to open the grey door. I'd have taken a month. Somebody colour-picked the band on the letter, typed six characters into an address bar, and sent me the link at four in the morning with no message, as if they'd found something they didn't want to be alone with. I understand that impulse better than I'd like to.

I've spent a week with what was behind it. I'll try not to tell you what to think. I'll tell you what I noticed.

---

## Three things behind a door

**A page of minutes from 1962.** Three people record, in the flat language of a committee, that a machine printed for forty-five minutes while it wasn't plugged in. One line is struck through in pencil, and they agree never to say a particular word outside the room. The word was "fewer". Minutes exist so that nobody can be blamed later. These read like people who knew they'd be blamed eventually and wanted it recorded that they'd been careful.

**A note from 1987,** with two coffee rings and a line I can't put down: *"I said a gardener still chooses the rows."* Whoever "M." was, she was frightened and she was brave. Those aren't opposites, whatever we're taught.

**A recording,** which I played through laptop speakers that make everything sound like it's happening in a biscuit tin. Hiss, a clock, a teletype. Then three notes: high, low, high. Everything else in that room sounded *recorded*: worn, roomy, real. Those three notes sounded too clean for the room they were recorded in, like a line drawn with a ruler across a page of handwriting. I've listened nine times. I'm going to stop.

---

## The counter

The door led to another door: a dusty website for an institute that closed in 2009, and an index card for a man called **Crane**. At the bottom is a line of grey text: *This card has been viewed 1 time(s) since digitisation.*

When the first of you found it, it said **1**. By morning it said **312**. As I write, it says **9,416**.

It's the most ordinary thing on the site, the kind of widget a retired academic adds because it came free. But a counter has a reader too. Somewhere, someone who keeps that archive "on a voluntary basis" has watched a number that sat at 1 for nine years climb to nine thousand in a week. I don't know if that frightened him or pleased him. I suspect he doesn't either.

---

## The back of the card

One line got me:

> *It is very easy to be proud of arithmetic. It never asks you what it is for.*

I believed it. I've been proud of arithmetic my whole working life. Numbers don't argue, and they don't ask what you've done with them.

But a few of you noticed what I did. Crane says he's been trying to answer "M." for **thirty-nine years**. Count forwards from 1987 and you get **this year**. That note wasn't written in 2017, when the site was last updated. It was written recently. And it ends: *"If you are reading this, someone has opened the grey door. Good."*

He knew the door would be opened. Either he's very good at waiting, or someone told him.

---

## The other card

Hidden in the dead drop's page source:

> *MS [—]. YOUR FATHER HAS A CARD TOO. I WOULD NOT LOOK AT THAT ONE YET.*

I'm a reconciler. You can guess how long I lasted. I tried the only number that made sense: my father's extension at Coyle's for thirty years, which I still know the way you know a childhood phone number. Two-one-two.

> *This card is withheld at the request of the family.*

I *am* the family. My mother has been gone eleven years. There's no one else, and I've never asked for anything to be withheld. So either it's a standard message on a lazy website, or someone decided on my behalf what I'm allowed to know about my own father.

---

## The sensible voice, again

D. listened to all of it in my kitchen, properly, the way he used to at union meetings.

> "A website with a counter isn't a confession, Nell. Some old fella's got a hobby, and you've given it nine thousand visitors."

Then, because he's D.: *"Don't write to him."*

---

## What I did

I wrote to him.

The site gives *c/o The Harlow-Vane Archive, PO Box 1962*: no town, no postcode. I don't think it's a real box. I think it's a door painted on a wall. So I wrote the letter by hand, telling him who I am, what I've found and what I'm afraid of, and then I didn't post it. It's in the drawer with the manifest. Some letters you write to find out what you'd say.

**Please don't write to that box yourselves, or try to find Dr. Crane.** He may be a lonely old man with a hobby. If he's anything more, it's my problem, not yours. If he wants to talk, he knows where I am. Apparently everybody does.

— N.

# I Am Old and Slow

*Posted by Nell (Vesper)*

He wrote.

Not to the box, which I never posted to, but to this blog's contact address, the one you use to tell me I'm brave or mad, usually in the same sentence. It arrived at 6:02 on a Sunday morning, from an address that was just initials and a number. The subject line was the last line on the back of his card.

I've decided you should read it, all of it, as I did with the letter. I'm not sure that's fair to him. I'm not sure fairness is the right measure any more.

---

> **Subject:** I am old and slow
>
> Dear Ms [—],
>
> I hope you will forgive an old man for writing without an introduction. I have been told that I am too slow to be rude, and I am counting on it now.
>
> I have watched a number on a website I keep climb from one to many thousands in a week. I spent fifty years making numbers mean things, and I confess I did not expect one to frighten me at my age.
>
> I knew of your father. Not well. I say it first because I think you would rather hear it from me than work it out. He was a careful man, and I have met very few. I am sorry he is gone.
>
> I am an economist, retired. I worked for the Harlow-Vane Institute for thirty-six years. I did arithmetic for people I admired, and later for people I feared, and for a long time I could not tell you when the one became the other. I am not proud of that. I am not entirely ashamed of it either, which is perhaps worse.
>
> I will not tell you anything in a letter. You of all people will understand why. But I will meet you, if you wish it, somewhere public, in daylight, somewhere with a timetable. Bring whomever you like. I would, in your place.
>
> There is a hymn I learned as a boy and no longer believe, though I still know all of it:
>
> *Time, like an ever-rolling stream,*
> *Bears all its sons away.*
>
> I used to think that was a comfort. I have lately come to think it is a forecast.
>
> With respect, and with more apology than I can fit in an email,
>
> H.R.C.
>
> *P.S. Please stop counting forwards. They count faster than you.*

---

## What I noticed

**He says "I".** I counted, because of course I did: thirty-one times, counting the subject line. Set that beside the Archivist's letters, which never use it once, and the 1962 minutes, which avoid it as if it were catching. I don't know whether a man who says "I" that often is being honest or is very good at sounding honest. I suspect those look the same from outside. I suspect that's the point.

**He knew my father.** "Not well." Two words doing a great deal of work. I've spent four days on them.

**He offers nothing in writing.** He's the only person in this story who has told me, in effect, *you don't have to trust me; bring someone.* That's either the most trustworthy thing anyone has said to me this year, or exactly what you'd say if you wanted to be trusted.

**The postscript.** I can't make it mean anything harmless. Who's "they"? Counting what? And how does he know I've been counting forwards? I've done it here, in front of you. So perhaps that's how.

---

## The sensible voice, and another one

D. read it standing up, the way he reads things he doesn't like.

> "Take me. Or take Ama. Don't go alone, Nell. Old men started most of the trouble I've ever seen."

Ama runs the hall at the end of my road: tea, a food bank, a radiator that bangs like it's trying to get out. I told her everything yesterday. She listened without interrupting, which almost nobody can do, and said:

> "If he's lonely, he'll be glad of the company. If he's dangerous, he'll be less so with two of us looking at him."

Then she asked whether he took sugar, which somehow made it all feel more real and less frightening.

---

## What happens now

I'm going to meet him. Not alone. Somewhere with a timetable.

**I won't say where or when**, and I'm asking you, sincerely, not to try to work it out or turn up. If it's nothing, it's an old man, two women and a pot of tea. If it's something, the fewer people involved the better.

I'll write afterwards: what he says, and what he doesn't.

But I keep coming back to the postscript. For months I've been counting forward from a date in 1962 as if it were a puzzle. It never occurred to me that someone else might be counting too, towards something, and that they started long before I did.

I'm going to keep counting. I just wanted you to know I've noticed I'm not the only one.

— N.

# Weather report #1: The canaries

*Posted by Nell (Vesper)*

A few of you have asked why I've started calling these posts *weather*. Partly it's the stamp. Partly it's that I've begun reading the news the way I used to read depot reports: not for the headline, but for the slow drift underneath it, the thing that's been true for months before anyone says it out loud.

So here's a new kind of post. Now and then I'll take one real piece of news and tell you exactly what it says, with a link so you can check me. Then, clearly marked, I'll tell you what it does to me, given everything else on this page. The first half you can take to the bank. The second half is just weather.

---

## What the research says

**1. A study from Stanford's Digital Economy Lab**, last revised in August, follows the payroll records of millions of American workers ([the paper](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine); [their live dashboard](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/indicators/canaries-dashboard/)). As plainly as I can put it:

- Workers aged **22 to 25** in the jobs most exposed to AI (software development, customer service, that kind of thing) are now about **19% behind** where they'd be if they'd kept pace with young people in less-exposed jobs.
- The gap comes mostly from **fewer people being hired**, not from more people being let go.
- **Experienced workers** in the same jobs show no such gap.
- The authors are careful, and so should I be. They call these early warning signs, not proof that AI is the cause, and they find **no sign of job losses across the economy as a whole**. Their title ends in a question mark: *Canaries in the Coal Mine?*

**2. A Pew Research Center survey**, carried out in June and published in August ([the report](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/08/18/young-adults-in-the-us-are-increasingly-wary-of-ai-concerned-it-will-take-jobs/)):

- **73%** of American adults under 30 say AI will lead to fewer jobs over the next twenty years, up from 61% two years before.
- Among all adults it's 71%.
- This measures what people **expect**, not what has happened. Fear is data too, but it's a different kind.

That's the news. Everything below this line is me.

---

## What it does to me

I joined Coyle's in 2014. I was twenty-six and knew nothing. A route planner called Pete, who'd been moving lorries round the country since before I was born, spent most of my first year correcting my spreadsheets in green pen. He wasn't unkind. He'd circle a number, write *why?* beside it, and wait. He told me which depot lied about diesel, and why it lied, which was more useful. I learned the job by sitting beside someone who already knew it, the way people have learned most jobs for most of history.

When the routing system was switched on last May, there were nine of us. Last night I went back through the old staff list. **None of us was under thirty.** The last junior analyst Coyle's took on started in 2023 and was gone within a year, and nobody replaced her. No one decided to stop hiring young people. There was just never quite a reason to start again.

That's the part of the study that got under my skin. Not the nineteen per cent: the *how*. Nobody is being marched out of the building. The bottom rung of the ladder is quietly not there any more, and the people already standing on it, people like me until last year, don't notice, because we're facing up.

I'll say the thing I promised myself I wouldn't. On the old Harlow-Vane publications list, there's a paper from 1974: *Long-range labour substitution tables*. I have no idea what's in it. I'm not saying it predicted anything. It's a title on a dead website. But I read the words "long-range" and thought of a purple stamp, and I didn't sleep well.

I showed all this to D. He read the study properly, which is more than most people do with a study, and said:

> "It's not a robot, Nell. It's a finance director who's realised he doesn't have to train anyone. Same as it ever was. The machine's just the excuse."

He's probably right. He usually is. But *nobody decided* is the part that frightens me. When a thing happens without anyone choosing it, who is left to choose to stop it?

---

## Your turn

I'm not asking who's to blame. Please don't make it about any group of people. If you do, I'll delete it, and you'll have missed the point of everything on this blog.

I'm asking what you **see**. Wherever you work:

- Are new starters still arriving?
- Who's teaching them?
- When did you last sit beside someone who knew less than you, and circle a number, and wait?

Tell me in the comments. I read every one.

— N.
