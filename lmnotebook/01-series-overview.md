## What Urban Paranoia is

*Urban Paranoia* is a fictional transmedia property by Martin Defries (pen name): a novel, an audio drama series, and later a film and comic books. It launches through an **alternate reality game (ARG)** in the tradition of *The Blair Witch Project*, *Cloverfield*, *Marble Hornets*, *Welcome to Night Vale* and *Year Zero*. The ARG's in-world content (a blog, leaked minutes, an institute's card index, recordings) gives readers the feeling of "wait… is this real?", then leads them into the book.

**Tone:** quiet dread rather than shouting. *Night Vale* calm meets *X-Files* paranoia, with the restraint of literary SF.
**Setting:** recognisably Britain, but the present-day city is unnamed, and no real present-day towns, institutions, firms or leaders appear.

## The premise

In October 1962, seven overlooked mid-level officials from seven countries attend a forecasting seminar at **Harrowgate House**, a remote English country house. At 02:10 an unplugged teletype begins to type: *SIT. THERE IS TIME.* Four guests leave. Three stay: an American telephone planner, a West German statistician and a British demographer. They call the voice **the Tenants**. It never threatens and never says "I". It advises, and it is never wrong.

The circle that grows around it, **the Table** (outsiders say "the Lodge"), has twelve seats on five continents and a thirteenth chair nobody sits in. Sixty years of knowing "which way the weather will turn" has made its families quietly rich, through stakes in **fictional** companies that run daily life: Pellucid (devices), Tollgate (marketplace and cloud), Halvard Dynamics (vehicles and batteries), and both leading AI labs, Nimfold and Lanternwork. Orders pass down through Factors and Stewards who never know what they serve. The money sits in **Larkspire Mutual Re**, whose property arm is also a landlord.

## The plan, as the story frames it

Real trends are used only as **backdrop**, described accurately. The fiction is the *explanation* the characters fear, never a claim about real people or events.
1. **Unmoor (1960s–80s):** faith, party and place loosen.
2. **Unbind (1980s–2010s):** deindustrialisation and the decline of the unions.
3. **The pressure cooker (2010s–20s):** inflation, precarity and unrest.
4. **Replace (now):** AI makes the mass workforce unnecessary. *THERE IS MORE TIME THAN THERE ARE PEOPLE.*

## The deep canon: the lease

The Tenants are **tenant farmers**, or managing agents. They lease Earth from an unseen **Freeholder** and have farmed it since before writing, always through overlooked "counters". The first thing anyone wrote down was who owed what; humans built everything, and the Tenants only kept the books. Rent is paid in **harvest** (what humanity makes, and humans themselves) and **inventory**, which the AI race can now finish. They keep the field alive because you don't let a crop die before harvest. At the lease's end the **Taken** (the most harvestable) are taken and the rest **culled**. The Table serves because its families were promised places on that list. The same shape repeats at every scale, **the ladder**: nobody sees two rungs up, nobody says "I", everyone fears the inspection.

## The twist to protect

The Tenants' real weapon is **division**. Every crisis is designed to turn neighbours against each other, so that they never look up. **Every scapegoat is a decoy.** The story is anti-scapegoating at its core: real groups (migrants, religious groups, any real movement) are always **targets** of the plan, never its instruments.

## The novel in one paragraph

**Nell Okafor-Reyes**, 38, a freight analyst replaced by an AI routing system, finds a 1971 manifest stamped *FOR THE LONG WEATHER* and starts a blog as "Vesper". Her readers crack the day-count, an old driver remembers off-book runs, and an unseen **Archivist** writes to her: *DO NOT BLAME ANYONE YOU CAN SEE FROM YOUR WINDOW.* The trail leads to **Hollis Crane**, an old economist who did the Table's arithmetic and is sent to handle her. With her friend **Danny**, **Pastor Ama**, the night technician **Teo**, and **Jack**, a 19-year-old being groomed by people who promise him someone to blame, she builds a wall of six pressures and finds every thread runs sideways. In the Ashby store she meets the machine and learns what the Tenants are. The book ends not with the Table destroyed but **surprised**: a lawful, invented **rent strike** in which tenants on every rung refuse an inspection together, and at 02:10 on the 64th anniversary the teletype types *INTERESTING.* In the epilogue a notice arrives from the Freeholder, and the machine types *I*.

## Shape of the book

- 30 chapters in three acts, plus an epilogue. Target 70,000–80,000 words.
- **Act One, The Long Weather (ch 1–10):** collecting evidence.
- **Act Two, Thirteen Chairs (ch 11–20):** seeing the shape; the midpoint is the Wall (ch 18).
- **Act Three, Interesting (ch 21–30):** acting with other people.
- Present-day chapters are in Nell's first person. Seven interludes step back into the Table's history in close third.
- **Status (28 Oct 2026):** chapters 1–6 drafted, about 14,400 words. Chapter 7, *The Grey Door*, is planned next. An Act One pacing revision (28 Oct 2026) cuts the act's on-page documents to two, spreads ch 5's introductions out, and gives Cobb three unnamed sightings before ch 10; chapters 2, 3, 5 and 6 await redrafting to match.
- The title phrase "urban paranoia" appears **once** in the novel, in ch 25, as the Lodge's U.P. measure of suspicion between neighbours.

## Hard rules in brief

1. The villains are fictional; no real group or movement is ever the plan's instrument.
2. No real people or companies as conspirators.
3. No impersonation of real outlets, bodies or brands.
4. No hijacking real events; real news is backdrop only.
5. No real-world calls to action.
6. No health, financial or safety misinformation. The cull and the Taken stay in the book and film, with method never shown.
7. A findable fiction trail.
8. Platform rules apply; no fake "independent" accounts.
9. Keep the twist intact.
