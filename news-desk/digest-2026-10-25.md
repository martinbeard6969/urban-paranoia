## Candidates

### 1. Young workers in AI-exposed jobs fall further behind (Stanford, Aug 2026 revision)
- **Source:** Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, *Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of AI*, Stanford Digital Economy Lab, revised 12 Aug 2026 ([paper page](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine); [dashboard](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/indicators/canaries-dashboard/))
- **Summary:** Using ADP payroll data through June 2026, workers aged 22–25 in the most AI-exposed occupations are about **19% below** where they'd be had they kept pace with less-exposed peers. Earlier versions reported 13% (Aug 2025) and 16% (Nov 2025). The effect comes mainly from **less hiring, not more firing**. Experienced workers show no comparable gap. The authors call these early descriptive indicators, not causal proof, and find **no economy-wide displacement**.
- **Beat:** AI and automation
- **Rating:** 🟢 Peer-tracked research, no individuals blamed, describable accurately.
- **Proposed use:** Weather report; plot seed
- **Story link:** Vesper's own layoff (Day 22,861); Harlow-Vane's 1974 *Long-range labour substitution tables*; the Tenants' "hands" counsel
- **Danny's check:** Passes. It's about firms' hiring incentives, not about any group.
- **Earliest use date:** now (broke 12 Aug + 4 weeks = 9 Sep)

**Key claims (verified?):**
- "Young workers in AI-exposed jobs are seeing 16% slower employment growth" (EO). ✅ Matches the Nov 2025 revision, **now superseded by 19%** (Aug 2026). Videos quote 13%, 16% or 19% depending on which version they read. **Always cite the current revision.**
- "73% of under-30s believe AI will lead to fewer jobs" (CNBC). ✅ Pew, 18 Aug 2026.

**Angles and framings:**
- **The "career ladder" losing its bottom rungs.** Entry-level work is the "book knowledge" AI does well. Tacit, social and strategic skills come with experience, which young people can no longer get (EO).
- **The training gap:** firms under-hire and under-train juniors because trained staff can leave for rivals. Private incentive and social need pull apart (EO).
- **History in both directions:** technology usually creates more jobs overall (CNBC panel guest; IBM chapters: horses, ATMs, Jevons paradox). But the Luddites were *skilled* workers. Knowledge workers may be this era's equivalent (EO).
- **AI in the hiring process itself:** students report automated screening making internships harder to get (CNBC).
- **Anxiety vs hope:** presenters repeatedly acknowledge the anxiety while urging optimism.

**Audience mood (themes only):** comment sections weren't reviewed this week.

**Questions people keep asking:**
- Is it really AI, or interest rates and the economy?
- If entry-level jobs disappear, how does anyone get experience?
- Will new jobs appear fast enough for the people affected now?
- Should I still go to university?

**Counter-arguments (for Danny):**
- The paper itself finds no economy-wide displacement.
- Forrester projects a modest net effect to 2030.
- Pew measures fear, not losses.
- Every technology wave has prompted "this time is different" (IBM).
- Hiring freezes also reflect the economic cycle.

**Red-filter notes:**
- IBM is an AI vendor with an interest in the "AI creates jobs" message, so treat it as advocacy.
- The Anthropic exposure study surfaced in the scan was **not used**. Anthropic makes the model running this desk, so prefer the independent Stanford work.

**Short quotes (≤25 words, attributed, timestamped):**
- "the young workers in those jobs are seeing 16% slower employment growth." (Bharat Chandar, EO, 0:00–0:06)
- "I do not blame 20 somethings for being anxious about the future." (Steve Liesman, CNBC Television, 5:49)

**What this adds to the story:**
- **Vesper's Weather report "The canaries":** she notices her old team at Coyle's had no one under 30, and no junior had been hired since 2023. She links the Stanford paper and Pew, then says what it makes her *feel*: the ladder she climbed has been quietly removed behind her. The fear is hers, and the facts stay accurate.
- **Plot seed:** Harlow-Vane's 1974 *labour substitution tables* can be shown (in the book) to predict "attrition at the point of entry". Fictional, and a chilling echo of the real finding's mechanism (less hiring, not more firing).
- **Danny line:** "It's not a robot, Nell. It's a finance director who's realised he doesn't have to train anyone."
