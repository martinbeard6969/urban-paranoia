# Weather report #1: The canaries

*Posted by Nell (Vesper)*

A few of you have asked why I've started calling these posts *weather*. Partly it's the stamp. Partly it's that I've begun reading the news the way I used to read depot reports: not for the headline, but for the slow drift underneath it, the thing that's been true for months before anyone says it out loud.

So here's a new kind of post. Now and then I'll take one real piece of news and tell you exactly what it says, with a link so you can check me. Then, clearly marked, I'll tell you what it does to me, given everything else on this page. The first half you can take to the bank. The second half is just weather.

---

## What the research says

**1. A study from Stanford's Digital Economy Lab**, last revised in August, follows the payroll records of millions of American workers ([the paper](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine); [their live dashboard](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/indicators/canaries-dashboard/)). As plainly as I can put it:

- Workers aged **22 to 25** in the jobs most exposed to AI (software development, customer service, that kind of thing) are now about **19% behind** where they'd be if they'd kept pace with young people in less-exposed jobs.
- The gap comes mostly from **fewer people being hired**, not from more people being let go.
- **Experienced workers** in the same jobs show no such gap.
- The authors are careful, and so should I be. They call these early warning signs, not proof that AI is the cause, and they find **no sign of job losses across the economy as a whole**. Their title ends in a question mark: *Canaries in the Coal Mine?*

**2. A Pew Research Center survey**, carried out in June and published in August ([the report](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/08/18/young-adults-in-the-us-are-increasingly-wary-of-ai-concerned-it-will-take-jobs/)):

- **73%** of American adults under 30 say AI will lead to fewer jobs over the next twenty years, up from 61% two years before.
- Among all adults it's 71%.
- This measures what people **expect**, not what has happened. Fear is data too, but it's a different kind.

That's the news. Everything below this line is me.

---

## What it does to me

I joined Coyle's in 2014. I was twenty-six and knew nothing. A route planner called Pete, who'd been moving lorries round the country since before I was born, spent most of my first year correcting my spreadsheets in green pen. He wasn't unkind. He'd circle a number, write *why?* beside it, and wait. He told me which depot lied about diesel, and why it lied, which was more useful. I learned the job by sitting beside someone who already knew it, the way people have learned most jobs for most of history.

When the routing system was switched on last May, there were nine of us. Last night I went back through the old staff list. **None of us was under thirty.** The last junior analyst Coyle's took on started in 2023 and was gone within a year, and nobody replaced her. No one decided to stop hiring young people. There was just never quite a reason to start again.

That's the part of the study that got under my skin. Not the nineteen per cent: the *how*. Nobody is being marched out of the building. The bottom rung of the ladder is quietly not there any more, and the people already standing on it, people like me until last year, don't notice, because we're facing up.

I'll say the thing I promised myself I wouldn't. On the old Harlow-Vane publications list, there's a paper from 1974: *Long-range labour substitution tables*. I have no idea what's in it. I'm not saying it predicted anything. It's a title on a dead website. But I read the words "long-range" and thought of a purple stamp, and I didn't sleep well.

I showed all this to D. He read the study properly, which is more than most people do with a study, and said:

> "It's not a robot, Nell. It's a finance director who's realised he doesn't have to train anyone. Same as it ever was. The machine's just the excuse."

He's probably right. He usually is. But *nobody decided* is the part that frightens me. When a thing happens without anyone choosing it, who is left to choose to stop it?

## Your turn

I'm not asking who's to blame. Please don't make it about any group of people. If you do, I'll delete it, and you'll have missed the point of everything on this blog.

I'm asking what you **see**. Wherever you work:

- Are new starters still arriving?
- Who's teaching them?
- When did you last sit beside someone who knew less than you, and circle a number, and wait?

Tell me in the comments. I read every one.

— N.
